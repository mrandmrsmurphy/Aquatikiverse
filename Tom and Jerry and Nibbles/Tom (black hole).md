**Tom** is a high-spin Kerr black hole residing at the center of the compact spheroidal remnant DGS-17, a disrupted dwarf galaxy located ~6 kiloparsecs from a galactic center. With a mass of ~5×10⁶ solar masses, Tom is a low-luminosity, accreting supermassive black hole with a compact, weakly accreting source and a planetary system. It is part of the [[Tom and Jerry and Nibbles]] system, and hosts [[Jerry (gas giant)]], which in turn hosts [[Nibbles (habitable moon)]].

Tom is bright compared with a star but extremely faint compared with its Eddington luminosity. The previous approximately 10²⁶ W subsolar model is superseded. The luminosity is a target power budget, not proof of a thin-disk spectrum or a particular accretion rate. Even a nominal efficiency of 0.1 would require approximately 8.5×10¹³ kg/s of accretion; a radiatively inefficient flow needs more mass for the same output.

A low-Eddington hot flow can produce substantial hard radiation. A persistently cool, radiatively efficient thin disk cannot simply be assumed at this ratio. The spectral energy distribution, any reprocessing component, angular emission, absorbing columns, material extent, variability, and particle outflow must be modeled together. [Accretion-flow framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.0586).

The habitability requirement is that enough optical/infrared radiation reaches Nibbles while the ionizing and particle components deposit most of their energy above the inhabited surface. The adopted bolometric luminosity establishes available heat, not radiation safety. If a physically realizable flow cannot meet that spectral and dose requirement, the spectrum, shielding, habitat depth, or luminosity must be revised. The description remains an explicit astrophysical hypothesis.

## Appearance

From Jerry’s working 50 AU orbit, the nonrotating gravitational-shadow reference diameter is approximately 0.59°. Spin and inclination change its outline. This lensing shadow is distinct from the much smaller coordinate horizon, whose apparent scale cannot be obtained by simply drawing its physical radius.

A compact luminous accretion structure surrounds the dark region, with potential Doppler brightness asymmetry and lensed background images. Its visible angular extent depends on where optical emission and reprocessing occur. The supplied artwork is illustrative; the image does not establish unaided visibility of background-star arcs.

Low-power polar jets and episodic nonthermal radio or line emission remain part of the setting. Their angular width, precession, and orientation must keep hazardous outflow away from the planets in the current epoch. The planet and moon travel in a low-density orbital region outside the dense accretion material; an extremely dilute magnetized plasma can still interact with Jerry.

From Nibbles, Jerry remains fixed in the sky because the moon is synchronously rotating. Tom moves through an approximately 46.3-hour direct-illumination cycle, with eclipses and source variability superimposed. Jerry’s own spin is independent of this motion. Visible, infrared, ultraviolet, and particle brightness must be distinguished when describing shadows, aurorae, and energy capture.

## History

Long before the present-day spiral structure of its host galaxy had fully assembled, in the crowded stellar fields of a dense protocluster, a colossal O-type star flared into being. Its mass likely exceeded several hundred solar masses — a brief and unstable behemoth of a star, born in a high-pressure region amid converging molecular clouds. The cluster, already massive, was undergoing runaway collapse in its core, and Tom’s progenitor emerged near the center of that process, surrounded by other massive stars spiraling inward through mass segregation.

Due to its overwhelming luminosity and rapid nuclear burning, the star’s life was measured in mere millions of years. It shed mass via powerful stellar winds, but not fast enough to prevent its core from collapsing. At the end, it may have bypassed a bright supernova altogether and undergone direct collapse into a black hole. In any case, the stellar core collapsed, leaving behind a stellar-mass black hole at the core of the young cluster.

For the next several hundred million years, Tom existed as a modest black hole, but its environment remained active. The dense stellar cluster around it — chaotic, collisional, and gas-rich — provided ample fuel. Nearby stars passed close enough to be tidally disrupted, feeding the growing hole with stellar debris. It also accreted remnant gas from the birth cloud of the cluster, as well as the debris of smaller black holes and neutron stars it absorbed through gravitational encounters.

Accretion and mergers changed both mass and angular momentum. Coherent accretion can spin a hole up, while other histories change or reduce its spin; the near-maximal value here remains a setting assumption. Tom’s mass eventually surpassed 10⁶ M☉ and reached approximately 5×10⁶ M☉ as the galaxy evolved.

Tom’s stellar cluster did not survive intact. It belonged to a small satellite dwarf galaxy that strayed too close to a more massive spiral system during the host galaxy’s assembly. Tidal stripping peeled away stars from the outskirts, and over billions of years, the dwarf was absorbed into the galactic halo. What remained — its dense nucleus, containing Tom and the core stellar population — drifted into a decaying orbit around the host galaxy.

Today, Tom resides about 6 kiloparsecs from the galactic center, embedded within the remnant nucleus of its birth system. This remnant is catalogued as DGS-17 (Dynamical Ghost Satellite 17), a compact spheroidal object only tens of parsecs across, sparsely populated and starved of gas. It retains the kinematic memory of its merger, orbiting the galaxy’s center with a retrograde inclination and high eccentricity — a fossil in motion.

For long spans of cosmic time, Tom has been quiescent or only weakly active, accreting at a tiny fraction of its Eddington rate. Occasional infall events — disrupted stars, clumps of gas, minor black hole mergers — have produced brief flaring episodes in the past, but these are rare in the current epoch.

Jerry originated in a K-dwarf binary environment with a primordial moon system that already included Nibbles and its ancient sub-ice biosphere. Later gravitational encounters transferred the planetary subsystem into a bound orbit around Tom. Binary exchange, additional encounters, and gas-mediated orbital evolution are candidate stages; their detailed trajectory and energy loss remain unmodeled.

The source’s destroyed K-dwarf is retained as a later event in the parent stellar system’s evolution, after Jerry and its moons had detached. It is not assumed that a single deep, star-disrupting pericenter also preserved the present large moon system. A passing molecular cloud may have perturbed the precursor trajectory but is not itself a complete capture mechanism.

The present reference orbit is nearly circular at 50 AU. Orbital energy loss, circumplanetary heating, moon retention, accretion-state history, and the capture epoch remain quantitative research tasks. The >2 Gyr age belongs to life’s full history, including its original environment; it is not a claim of >2 Gyr survival at the present orbit and luminosity.

Jerry and Nibbles persist outside the dense accretion material. Their revised geometry leaves room for a rocky moon outside its disruption boundary, while a larger radiative budget supports a candidate temperate climate. Atmospheric absorption, particle transport, chemical productivity, and long-term tidal evolution remain to be demonstrated; no gigayear stability certification is claimed.

Tom, now in a long-lived low-luminosity state, burns with energy never its own, the compact engine at the heart of a phantom galaxy remnant — a dark core from an early epoch, still shaping the fates of bodies drawn into its reach.

## Model status and remaining work

The setting’s proposed diagnostics include stellar dynamics, reverberation or timing studies, X-ray/UV variability, weak iron emission, and radio/IR continuum measurements. These are fictional inference methods and spectral targets, not observations of a real Tom.

**Nibbles** is a habitable, terrestrial-class moon orbiting the warm super-Jovian planet [[Jerry (gas giant)|Jerry]] within the gravitational well of the low-mass supermassive black hole [[Tom (black hole)|Tom]]. Tidally locked to Jerry and orbiting in approximately 44.76 hours, Nibbles lies beyond the classical fluid Roche estimate and in the inner part of Jerry’s Hill sphere in the revised working architecture. It is notable for its dense nitrogen-dominated atmosphere, NOₓ-driven redox chemistry, and its survival within an extreme but surprisingly stable astrophysical environment.

## Physical Characteristics

Nibbles is approximately 0.7 times the radius of Earth and has a mass of 0.4 M⊕, placing it in the sub-Earth category. Its mean density of 6429 kg/m³ suggests an iron-rich composition, and its surface gravity (8.02 m/s²) is roughly 82% that of Earth — compatible with a substantial atmosphere under a suitable escape and replenishment history. Its native environment supports Pedonpods; its CO₂-, NOₓ-, and acid-bearing air is unsuitable for unprotected humans.

The escape velocity is approximately 8.46 km/s. Retention still depends on thermospheric heating, irradiation, sputtering, and atmospheric supply; escape speed does not establish long-term survival by itself. Nibbles has no known moons of its own and is rotationally locked to Jerry, perpetually showing the same face to its primary.

## Orbit and Illumination

The reference orbit is circular, prograde, and near Jerry’s equatorial plane at **1,000,000 km**, with a two-body period of approximately **44.76 hours**. Nibbles remains synchronously rotating: **Jerry is fixed in its sky**. Jerry itself retains an approximately 8-hour spin and orbits Tom at **50 AU** in approximately **57.75 days**.

For the circular coplanar reference, Tom’s direct-illumination cycle relative to a surface longitude is approximately **46.26 hours**. Tom rises, crosses the sky, and sets where local geometry permits. There is no permanent Tom-facing hemisphere. Jerry’s reflected illumination changes phase, while its thermal radiation supplies a small continuous near-side contribution.

Jerry subtends approximately **9.0°** from the moon’s center and slightly more at the sub-Jerry surface. Its cloud face, variable phase, ring aspect, and aurorae dominate the near-side sky. At favorable alignment, Jerry eclipses Tom; a point-source coplanar approximation gives a shadow duty cycle near 2.5% and a central eclipse around 1.2 hours. The source’s finite emitting size and orbital inclination change this.

The planet-facing hemisphere retains temperate plains and shallow seas in the working geography. The far side retains extensive ice, fissures, and geothermal aquifers. These differences depend mainly on terrain, ocean circulation, cloud/albedo feedbacks, and regional geothermal history; the approximately 0.175 W/m² normal incident intrinsic Jerry radiation at this orbit is too small to explain a global hemispheric climate split by itself. Both hemispheres experience direct Tom illumination and atmospheric heat transport.

**HNO₃** occurs as trace vapor and in aerosols/aqueous nitrate; aerosol abundance needs a particle or mass loading rather than an effective gas partial pressure. **O₃** is described by a provisional **10–30 DU column target**, not a molar fraction. Its survival alongside reactive nitrogen must be checked photochemically. Neither omitted trace gas is assigned a measured abundance by the normalized reference above; a detailed model must renormalize accordingly.

High-altitude radiation and particle deposition can dissociate N₂ and oxygen-bearing species, creating NOₓ, peroxide, and nitrates. Atmospheric mixing, precipitation, deposition, and organisms transport oxidants toward habitats. HNO₃ condensation and the balance of NO/NO₂, nitrate, nitrite, NH₃, and O₃ depend on temperature, water, pH, and reaction rates.

Low-light haze can scatter visible disk light; violet/green skies and curtains are attributed principally to airglow and auroral emission where the relevant excitation supports those colors. Ozone is an absorbing gas layer rather than an ozone cloud. The high-energy spectrum, atmospheric column, and field geometry must keep surface dose compatible with the stated biosphere.

## Habitability

*Main articles: [[Life on Nibbles]], [[Pedonpods]]*

**Habitability is the governing design requirement.** Tom’s 2,000 L☉ bolometric reference at 50 AU supplies approximately 0.80 F⊕ or 1,089 W/m² before eclipses and absorption. A Bond albedo of 0.30 and approximately 2.5% point-source eclipse duty give a globally averaged absorbed external flux near **186 W/m²**, corresponding to a stellar-equivalent effective temperature near **239 K**. A greenhouse of roughly **35–45 K** would place inhabited regional climates near **275–285 K**. This is an energy-consistent target, not a completed climate simulation.

The CO₂-rich 1.3-atm atmosphere and water feedback provide candidate greenhouse mechanisms. Spectral absorption, clouds, circulation, ice feedbacks, and source anisotropy determine whether the target is achieved. Tom’s luminosity is bolometric: the required optical/infrared fraction, the absorbed X-ray/UV fraction, and the surface dose are independent acceptance criteria. A spectrum delivering most power destructively to the upper atmosphere would not satisfy the habitat model merely because the total wattage is sufficient.

Radioactivity, geothermal circulation, and residual tidal dissipation sustain deep aquifers and local refugia. Tidal heating is not assumed negligible: it depends on the forced orbit and the moon’s viscoelastic and ocean response. A preliminary constant-Q diagnostic spans tenths to several W/m² for plausible choices, requiring dedicated refinement. Excessive heating would require another orbital or rheological revision rather than being declared harmless.

Radiation-driven nitrogen fixation supplies oxidant precursors. Native food webs consume nitrate/nitrite and related acceptors using methane, ammonia, hydrogen, organic matter, and reduced mineral donors. Peroxide and ONOO⁻ pathways are secondary, and O₂ remains a niche acceptor. Direct aurorotrophy is supplementary and still speculative; radiation resistance and a strong field are not evidence of unlimited productivity or safety.

The retained near-side plains and seas, far-side cold provinces, and deep aquifers form a regional mosaic rather than a permanently lit and permanently dark pair. Pedonpods follow resource fronts, variable irradiation, eclipses, and magnetic conditions within this geography. Habitable here means suitable for the native biosphere, with no claim of human-breathable air.

## Observational Notes

Jerry is fixed above the near-side horizon, reaching approximately 9° across near the sub-Jerry region. Its rotating bands, changing reflected phase, ring crossings, and auroral patches vary independently. The far-side surface lacks a direct view of Jerry.

Tom crosses both hemispheres’ skies through the approximately 46.3-hour illumination cycle. Its nonrotating shadow reference is approximately 0.59° across at 50 AU, surrounded by emission whose visible size depends on the actual accretion spectrum and geometry. A horizon is not directly pictured as a dark material surface. Background lensing is geometrically expected; visible arcs and “Einstein ripples” require suitable background sources, contrast, and resolving power, so unaided visibility is not assumed.

Eclipses, airglow, and aurorae punctuate the low-visible-light setting. A dim visible sky can coexist with substantial bolometric heating only if the source spectrum supports that split. There is no permanent direct-disk day, reflected twilight, or fixed Tom horizon position.

**Oxhydra** is a dry, oxidant-rich terrestrial exoplanet orbiting the K-type star [[BD−08°2823 System|BD−08°2823]] at a distance of about 0.56 astronomical units. Slightly smaller and less massive than Earth, Oxhydra supports a chemosynthetic biosphere driven by hydrogen peroxide and methane-based metabolisms rather than oxygenic photosynthesis. Its crust is dominated by magnesium oxides and peroxides, providing a long-term oxidant reservoir that substitutes for free molecular oxygen in most biological redox chemistry.

The planet presents a stark, high-albedo surface marked by pale oxidant crusts and dark volcanic belts. It is orbited by a single, captured retrograde moon, **Umbra**, whose nearly black carbonaceous surface provides a visual contrast to Oxhydra’s bright disk.

## Physical and orbital characteristics

Oxhydra has a mass of about 0.86 M🜨 and a radius of 0.96 R🜨, giving it a mean density of 5.37 g/cm³. These values imply an Fe–Mg silicate interior with a relatively thin, chemically distinctive crust enriched in oxidized magnesium phases. Surface gravity is approximately 9.2 m/s², slightly lower than Earth’s but within a similar range.

The planet orbits BD−08°2823 on a nearly circular path with an orbital period of roughly 178 Earth days. Its semimajor axis of 0.56 AU gives about 0.717 F🜨 at the working stellar luminosity; habitability depends on its specialized atmosphere. A rotation period of about 27 hours produces moderate day–night cycles without tidal locking. Insolation at the top of the atmosphere is lower than modern Earth’s, but this is offset by a substantial greenhouse contribution from hydrogen collision-induced absorption and methane, maintaining a global mean surface temperature near 260 K.

Oxhydra’s high Bond albedo (≈0.65) is set by reflective oxidant crusts, intermittent high-altitude hazes, and thin cloud decks over active volcanic and hydration zones. The combination of low insolation, high reflectivity, and substantial greenhouse forcing helps keep water activity episodic and spatially patchy.

## Atmosphere and climate

Oxhydra’s bulk atmosphere is reducing. The working model is several bars, with an illustrative 5-bar reference pressure, dominated by H₂ with CH₄ and subordinate N₂. Exact fractions and the pressure needed for the approximately 260 K mean remain climate-model parameters. Oxidizing conditions occur chiefly in mineral surfaces and isolated wet habitats; free atmospheric O₂ is only a trace. Volcanically derived sulfur gases (SO₂, H₂S) and minor nitrogen oxides contribute to both photochemistry and specialized metabolisms.

Water vapor is present at low average abundance but can spike locally during volcanic degassing events and near fumarolic systems that tap deep reservoirs. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is an important but minor atmospheric constituent, produced photochemically and released from the oxidant crust during hydration events. It is short-lived in the gas phase, tending to partition into brine films or adsorb onto mineral surfaces.

The climate is characterized by:

- Cold, clear highlands with very low water activity.
- Localized fogs and thin cloud decks above volcanic arcs and impact-modified basins.
- Episodic rains and brine films generated when steam plumes intersect cooler air masses.
- High-altitude hazes from sulfur photochemistry and organic aerosol formation.

There is no ocean-fed global hydrological cycle. Water circulates through regional vapor, frost, brine, and groundwater reservoirs. Evaporation redistributes water; net loss occurs only when hydrogen escapes after upper-atmospheric photochemistry. Deep degassing and slow groundwater recharge sustain selected habitats. Salts, peroxide content, and local geothermal warming determine which brines remain liquid near or below 260 K.

## Geology and mineralogy

Oxhydra’s lithosphere is dominated by ultramafic, magnesium-rich silicates derived from a forsteritic mantle. In irradiated surface layers, oxidant deposition and mineralization produce patchy MgO₂-bearing coatings on MgO-rich substrates. Their abundance varies with exposure, burial, and reactions with reduced gases. These minerals are metastable in dry conditions but release H₂O₂ when hydrated, forming the energetic backbone of the biosphere.

Major mineral groups include:

- **Forsterite (Mg₂SiO₄)** in the upper mantle and lower crust, acting as the main reservoir of Mg and Si.
- **Periclase (MgO)** and **magnesium peroxide (MgO₂)** in the upper crust, constituting the reactive oxidant-rich regolith.
- **Quartz (SiO₂)** in locally differentiated or altered silica-rich terrains, spatially separate from the dominant ultramafic assemblages.
- **Iron oxides** such as magnetite (Fe₃O₄) and hematite (Fe₂O₃), providing remanent crustal magnetism and secondary redox couples.
- **Manganese oxyhydroxides** (e.g. manganite, MnOOH), serving as trace metal sources for enzymatic analogs.
- **Apatite group phosphates** as the primary phosphorus reservoir, often biolimiting due to their sparse distribution.
- **Iron-bearing sulfates** such as jarosite and other oxidized salts in surface weathering zones.

Weathering on Oxhydra is largely driven by rare but intense interactions between liquid water, sulfides, and oxidant minerals. These events reshuffle magnesium, sulfur, iron, and phosphorus between mineral reservoirs, periodically exposing fresh reactive surfaces.

## Tectonics and surface environment

Despite its limited surface water inventory, Oxhydra exhibits active tectonics. Heat loss is accommodated by a combination of mantle convection and brittle crustal recycling, producing:

- Broad, stable highlands of oxidant-enriched crust where externally generated peroxide is captured and MgO₂-bearing coatings accumulate under dry conditions.
- Narrow belts of active rifting and subduction-like downwelling, where hydrated crust is buried and partially reduced.
- Volcanic arcs and hotspots that deliver H₂, CH₄, sulfur gases, and steam to the surface.

At the intersection of tectonics and volatiles, Oxhydra develops its characteristic **hydration fronts**. These are moving boundaries where newly released water infiltrates MgO₂-rich crust, triggering intense H₂O₂ production and transient warm brine environments. Between fronts lie extensive oxidant deserts: cold, dry surfaces with little ongoing chemistry.

Over geological time, impact events and mantle upwellings have generated fractured basins that trap brines in porous rock. In such regions, prolonged interaction between water, oxidant minerals, and organosilicon matter has produced composite materials known as **siloxyl soils**. These soils can retain water for extended periods, buffering redox conditions and supporting more continuous, though still patchy, biological activity.

## Umbra, the captured moon

Oxhydra is orbited by a single small satellite, **Umbra**, on a retrograde, moderately inclined orbit with a period of several days. Umbra is thought to be a captured outer-system body with a strongly carbonaceous composition. Its surface has a very low albedo (≲4%), dominated by:

- Amorphous carbon and graphitic basalts.
- Dark organic residues produced by irradiation and slow chemistry.
- Sparse, devolatilized minerals with little evidence for hydration.

Umbra’s possible environmental effects include:

- Modulating stress patterns in Oxhydra’s lithosphere, subtly affecting the timing and distribution of volcanic and fumarolic activity.
- Producing irregular eclipse cycles that alter local photochemical reaction rates and upper-atmosphere peroxide production.
- Possible dust redistribution, whose importance depends on the still-undetermined orbit and particle trajectories.

The visual contrast between Oxhydra’s pale oxidant crusts and Umbra’s near-black disk is a notable feature of the system.

## Biosphere

*Main article: [[Life on Oxhydra]]*

Oxhydra hosts a peroxide-based biosphere that operates within narrow windows of hydration and redox disequilibrium. The fundamental biological unit is the **bastion**, a brine-based carbon–organosilicon cell analog with silica-rich structures and a PDMS-derived composite envelope. Bastions form the basis of several major ecological guilds, including hydration catalysts, peroxidovores, peroxisynthetics, sulfur-metabolizers, and various auxiliary trace-metal specialists.

Life is concentrated along hydration fronts, volcanic caldera rims, and siloxyl soils where water, oxidants, and reduced gases coexist. In most regions the biosphere cycles between brief active phases and long dormant intervals, synchronizing metabolism with rare wet episodes.

In some impact-modified and tectonically fractured provinces, especially those affected by the so-called **Siloxyl Revolution**, the evolution of water-retaining siloxyl matrices and continuant macroforms has produced more nearly continuous ecosystems. Among these are the [[Pumper Beetles]], a clade of mobile, shell-bodied continuant peroxidovores that maintain internal brine and oxidant stores and move through subsurface siloxyl soils. Their complex chemical senses, environmental engineering, and cooperative behavior mark one of the most advanced expressions of Oxhydran life.

The reference atmosphere is a working greenhouse model, not a solved radiative–convective climate. With Bond albedo 0.65, stellar equilibrium is approximately 197 K; the 260 K surface target therefore needs substantial greenhouse warming. Hydrogen escape and replacement must be included.

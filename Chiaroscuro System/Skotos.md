**Skotos** is a massive, distant terrestrial-class planet in the outer [[Chiaroscuro]] planetary system, orbiting at a semimajor axis of approximately 11.0 AU. Skotos exhibits a unique planetary structure dominated by dense carbon-based compounds overlying an oversized iron core, together with a bright ring system, making it visually striking despite its extremely dark surface. In system classification it is regarded as a **mega-terrestrial** world.

## Physical Characteristics
Skotos has a mass of approximately 19.1 Earth masses and a radius of 1.9 R⊕, placing it well into the category of super-Earths or mega-terrestrials in mass. Its bulk density of 15.36 g/cm³ is unusually high; interior models suggest an oversized iron core under immense pressure, overlain by a thin silicate mantle and carbon-rich outer layers. The planet’s surface gravity reaches 52.1 m/s², more than five times that of Earth, allowing a small volatile inventory to be retained. A tenuous, variable atmosphere contains methane and other hydrocarbons, with locally supplied gas from outgassing. Soot, tholins, and carbon-black particles contribute to suspended haze and surface coatings. Pressure remains unspecified.

The surface composition is believed to include layered **polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)**, **iron sulfides**, and dense carbon allotropes such as graphite and possibly amorphous diamond-like phases. Low stellar flux at this distance—about 5% of the flux Earth receives from the Sun—and an extremely low geometric albedo of 0.02 render the surface virtually undetectable in reflected light. A working stellar-equilibrium estimate near 130 K gives weak thermal emission, making Skotos itself one of the darkest large bodies in the system.

## Rings and Moons
Despite the planet’s muted reflectivity, it is encircled by a luminous, **tilted ring system** composed primarily of reflective **water ice and silicate grains**, giving the rings a geometric albedo exceeding 0.5. These rings are optically prominent under backlighting or at opposition and are potential telescopic targets from [[Eirda]], with resolving power and collecting area set by the actual geometry. Two prominent natural satellites orbit the planet—high-albedo moons likely dominated by water ice and silicates—providing additional contrast against Skotos’s dark backdrop and casting moving, bright traces across the ring plane.

The contrast between the near-black planetary disk and its high-albedo rings and moons makes Skotos a high-impact target in scattered light imaging: the planet itself is almost invisible, while the ring–moon system outlines its presence as a bright, thin halo in the outer sky.

## Orbital and Systemic Context

Skotos’s reference semimajor axis is 11.0 AU with eccentricity near 0.03. Its nominal two-body period around the 2.2 M☉ binary is about 24.6 years and the reference circular speed about 13.3 km/s. The massive companions perturb those elements.

It lies outside Umbryl and the revised comet population near 8–10 AU. From Skotos, the central stars and inner planets occupy the inner sky, with the comet population extending inward from its orbit.

The bright rings are tilted to the planetary orbital plane and follow the locally preferred plane of an oblique rotating planet. This preserves their inclined appearance without requiring a persistent large displacement from the equilibrium Laplace plane. Ring radii, rotation, obliquity, and the two moons’ orbits require a dedicated satellite model.

## Scientific Significance
Skotos is of particular interest as a **low-albedo planetary body** with a **high-contrast ring system**, providing an excellent case study in mixed-albedo dynamics, cold-terrestrial chemistry, and system-wide visibility under extreme reflectivity gradients. Its dense interior, strong gravity, and peculiar composition challenge typical planetary classification schemes and offer insight into carbon-rich accretion regimes and iron-enhanced planet formation.

The combination of a nearly invisible planetary disk and brilliant rings also makes Skotos an important analogue for exoplanetary systems where rings or moons dominate the observable signal, complicating the interpretation of direct-imaging and phase-curve observations.

**Ereba** is a cold, methane-rich ice giant orbiting at 17.0 astronomical units in the [[Chiaroscuro]] planetary system. With a radius of approximately 5 Earth radii and a mass of 23 Earth masses, Ereba exhibits a mean density of roughly 1.01 g/cm³, consistent with a volatile-rich interior and a substantial hydrogen–helium envelope. Their relative masses remain model-dependent. At its distance from the ~6 L☉ Chiaroscuro binary, Ereba receives about 2.1% of the stellar flux that Earth receives from the Sun. Its equilibrium temperature is approximately 104 K, with methane, ethane, and other condensates distributed according to their local vapor abundance and pressure–temperature profile. Deeper layers include compressed water-, ammonia-, and carbon-bearing material above a rock-rich interior.

The planet's atmosphere is suffused with trace complex hydrocarbons and soots, derived from photochemical processes acting upon methane, yielding a high-altitude haze that renders Ereba's disk visually uniform, featureless, and exceptionally dark. With a planetary Bond albedo near 0.05 and a geometric albedo of only 0.05–0.15, Ereba reflects only a minute fraction of the incident starlight, appearing to distant observers as a muddy brown, violet, or gray orb depending on phase angle and observational wavelength. A high haze layer masks much of the weather below, producing only minor visible albedo variation under ordinary conditions. Its featureless appearance does not determine the strength of deep convection.

Beneath the visible atmosphere, models suggest a stratified interior: an outer molecular envelope of hydrogen, helium, and methane transitions into layers of water, ammonia, and methane ices under increasing pressure, eventually merging with a rock-rich, silicate–ice core. Radiogenic heating in the core and slow gravitational contraction provide a modest internal heat flux, which helps maintain the deep convective layers and sustains occasional localized upwellings that slightly perturb the overlying haze.

Encircling this somber planet is a sparse, nearly invisible dusty ring system composed chiefly of silicate and carbonaceous debris, likely sourced from micrometeoroid impacts on inner moons and the slow erosion of captured cometary fragments. The true visual drama arises from Ereba’s satellite swarm: over two dozen small, high-albedo icy moons are assigned compact prograde paths around the planet, subject to a satellite stability model. These satellites, composed of water ice, ammonia hydrates, and minor carbon dioxide ice, shine brightly against the dark backdrop of the central planet, creating, in resolved images, a shifting halo of “white bees” around a dark central disk. The juxtaposition of Ereba’s inert darkness with the sparkling liveliness of its moons establishes one of the most striking natural contrasts observable in the Chiaroscuro system.

From the skies of [[Eirda]], Ereba itself is typically dim and difficult to resolve, but its moons can produce subtle resolved changes and mutual occultations. Their combined contribution depends on total reflecting area and phase. Suitable high-resolution images can reveal the planet surrounded by moving satellite points. Long exposures require tracking or reconstruction to avoid smearing their motion.

## Orbital and Satellite Dynamics

Ereba’s reference orbit is 17.0 AU with eccentricity near 0.04. Its nominal period around the 2.2 M☉ binary is about 47.3 years and its circular reference speed about 10.7 km/s. Perturbations by Umbryl and Euryphessa modify its osculating elements.

The nominal Hill radius is about 55.6 million km at the semimajor axis and about 53.4 million km at perihelion. The described moon system occupies the inner few million km. An icy fluid satellite of roughly 1 g/cm³ has a Roche estimate near 78,000 km.

Over two dozen small moons are part of the setting. Their proposed prograde orbits, masses, eccentricities, inclinations, and resonant families need a satellite model. A large Hill sphere provides geometric room, while mutual encounters and tides determine actual survival.

Inner moons may complete orbits in under two days, while an orbit at 4 million km takes about 192 days. This range supports varied mutual-event timescales. Impacts, ring interactions, and tides slowly reshape the satellite population.



## Modeling notes

Low planetary albedo, a bland dark haze, and a swarm of reflective moons are the primary constraints. “Ice giant” supersedes the original sub-Neptunian label. The exact cloud inventory and the relative rock, volatile, and hydrogen–helium fractions remain uncertain.

The “white bees” description applies to appropriately resolved telescopic or spacecraft observations. Satellite sizes and observing geometry must be specified before assigning integrated brightness or telescope requirements.

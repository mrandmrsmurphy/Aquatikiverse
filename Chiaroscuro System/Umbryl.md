**Umbryl** is a cool brown dwarf, provisionally described as late-T/early-Y, in the [[Chiaroscuro]] planetary system, orbiting the central binary at 5.90 AU. It is the outermost massive object in the system before the cometary belt and functions as a transitional body between the inner and outer system. Its disk has very low visible reflectivity, and rare impact-driven bursts provide a much stronger infrared signature.

## Physical Characteristics
With a mass of 20.3 Jupiter masses, Umbryl lies well below the threshold for sustained hydrogen fusion but above the deuterium-burning limit of roughly 13 M_J. It likely experienced a brief phase of deuterium fusion early in its history; that phase ended billions of years ago, leaving a cooling, fully substellar object. Its interior is strongly compressed and partially supported by electron degeneracy pressure, giving it a compact, high-gravity structure. Its reflected brightness depends on atmospheric optical properties and viewing geometry.

Umbryl has a radius of 1.05 times that of Jupiter and a bulk density of about 2.2×10⁴ kg/m³ (~22 g/cm³). Surface gravity is extreme, around 460 m/s², compressing a deep hydrogen–helium atmosphere laced with methane and ammonia absorption bands. A representative cool outer-atmospheric layer is near 500 K. The effective temperature and radius must be matched to a 20.3 M♃ cooling model at the system’s age. Stellar irradiation at 5.90 AU would give an equilibrium temperature near 179 K at very low Bond albedo, allowing interior heat to dominate.

The atmosphere is dominated by hydrogen and helium, with methane and ammonia contributing spectral opacity, with additional opacity from water vapor and various metal-bearing species. With virtually no visible output and a Bond albedo below 0.01, its quiescent reflected-light signal is faint. Detectability from [[Eirda]] depends on visible geometric albedo, phase, and instrumental contrast. Its characteristic impact phenomena are primarily infrared.

## Emissions and Impact Events
Umbryl’s mass places it firmly in the brown dwarf regime, but at an age of several billion years any nuclear burning has long ceased. Its present luminosity arises from slow gravitational contraction and the release of stored thermal energy. In quiescence, Umbryl emits most strongly in the mid-infrared, with minimal flux at optical wavelengths.

Umbryl orbits just interior to the revised comet population near 8–10 AU. Its gravitational field perturbs nearby bodies into eccentric and sunward-skimming orbits, and a fraction of these are scattered inward toward the dwarf itself. This continual weak stirring helps maintain the belt’s dynamically excited structure and depletes unstable trajectories on a distribution of timescales.

When a large comet approaches Umbryl, its path is often unstable. Bodies with modest tensile strength can be tidally disrupted into chains before impact; stronger survivors plunge intact into the atmosphere at entry speeds comparable to its local escape speed, about 260 km/s, before atmospheric braking. In either case the result is dramatic.

As an infalling body strikes Umbryl’s upper atmosphere, compression and shock heating ionize the gas, with initial conditions much hotter than the later radiating plume. Cooling material passes through temperatures of thousands of kelvin. Water ice, ammonia, methane, and carbon monoxide vaporize and dissociate in milliseconds. The impact’s kinetic and gravitational energy powers the radiation. Dissociation consumes some energy and recombination returns part of it as the plume evolves.

Much of the cooling plume’s output emerges in the infrared. Large impacts produce strong transient contrast against Umbryl’s quiescent emission. Larger impactors can generate total radiative energies comparable to small stellar flares—brief, sharp spikes that fade within minutes. At the hottest point of the event, a shallow plume above the impact site may emit a faint, short-lived visible flash, produced by incandescent dust grains, ionized molecular fragments, and blackbody emission from the shock front.

To an observer on [[Eirda]], these events appear as infrared brightenings when monitored with instruments. Rare energetic impacts can also produce a brief visible spark. The usual events supply little visible illumination at Eirda. Afterward, the disturbed region cools and collapses. Transient thermal arcs or glowing layers can persist for hours, slowly dispersing as the atmosphere returns to radiative–convective equilibrium.

## Orbital Characteristics

Umbryl’s reference orbit is 5.90 AU with eccentricity near 0.02. Including its mass in the two-body estimate gives a period near 9.62 years and a reference relative speed near 18.3 km/s.

It remains outside Kharon and Eirda and inside the comet population and the dark outer worlds. The wider spacing supplies room for the massive brown dwarf’s gravitational influence. Perturbations from the other companions produce departures from fixed Keplerian elements.

The low-inclination planar model summarized in [[Chiaroscuro System]] supplies a preliminary dynamical screen. A full binary-plus-planets integration with specified inclinations remains separate work.

## Significance
As the only brown dwarf in the Chiaroscuro system, Umbryl acts as:

- a comet shepherd at the inner edge of the belt,
- a sink for destabilized planetesimals, and
- a source of rare but spectacular infrared flares in the outer sky.

The rare visible brightenings at Umbryl’s position have acquired a reputation on [[Eirda]] as ominous signs. Scientific study later revealed their cometary and gravitational origin, but the association with upheaval, erasure, and sudden change remains embedded in Eirdan traditions and symbolism.


## Modeling notes

The 5.90 AU orbit supersedes the earlier 3.02 AU position. Very low reflectivity, accretion-powered infrared bursts, and rare visible sparks are the main setting constraints. Effective temperature, intrinsic luminosity, and detailed spectrum require a brown-dwarf evolution and atmosphere model. The Sonora grids provide a starting point. [Sonora Bobcat models](https://zenodo.org/records/5063476)

Impactor sizes, radiative efficiency, plume geometry, and the rate of large impacts remain unspecified. The comet reservoir must be modeled as a perturbed population with a finite supply. Purple or violet renderings of emission arcs can use false-color infrared data.

**Mot** is a frozen ice giant at the distant outer edge of the [[Thrírlitir System]], orbiting all three stars (A, B, and C) at 117 AU. It sits far beyond the frost lines of the inner stars, in a zone of volatile ices and remnant planetesimals. It is not a gas giant: most of its ~30 Earth masses is rock and ice, wrapped in a thin hydrogen–helium envelope. Cold, slow, and almost unlit, it is the system's most extreme world, a planet of stacked ice-haze decks, long-lived frozen weather, and rare, enormous storms that release heat the interior has stored for centuries.

Starlight supplies ~0.069 W/m² averaged over the planet (equilibrium temperature 33.2 K for albedo 0.11). A further ~0.016 W/m² (~2.3×10¹⁴ W) leaks up from the interior, which brings the effective temperature to 35 K. The interior is stably stratified (compositional layering suppresses large-scale convection, as inferred for Uranus), so it releases heat only slowly and unevenly.

For the scientific community on [[Valen]], Mot is a frontier for cold-atmosphere chemistry and a philosophical boundary object: the last great planet before the system fades into true interstellar space.

## Orbital characteristics

Mot orbits the barycentre of the three stars at ~117 AU, with e ≈ 0.09 and an inclination of 1.6° to the system's invariable plane. The Keplerian period around the combined ~2.55 M☉ is 792.5 years, about 1,240 Valen-years. The inner stellar orbits (B–C at 0.3 AU, A–(B+C) at 7 AU) are far enough inside that the three stars act as a single mass at Mot's distance, giving a stable circumsystem orbit.

The Hill radius is ~2.7 AU at the semimajor axis and ~2.4 AU at periastron. Prograde satellites are stable to roughly half the Hill radius and retrograde ones farther, so the moon family sits comfortably inside it. Residual debris and small planetesimals in the surrounding region are scattered or shepherded by Mot over millions of years.

Orbital distance varies by ±9%, so absorbed flux varies by about ±18%, with equilibrium temperature 34.8 K at periastron and 31.8 K at apastron. With an 18° tilt, each hemisphere has a ~200-year summer and a ~200-year winter; polar nights last many decades to centuries.

## Physical characteristics

Mot has a mass of ~30 M⊕ and a mean (1-bar) radius of ~34,000 km. That gives a density of ~1.09 g/cm³, a surface gravity of 10.3 m/s², and an escape velocity of 26.5 km/s. This is within the range of Neptune-class planets (a mass–radius scaling of R ≈ 3.9 R⊕ (M/17 M⊕)^0.59 gives 5.4 R⊕). The composition is roughly 3–5 M⊕ of rock and iron, ~20 M⊕ of water–ammonia–methane ices (at depth a hot, ionic, electrically conducting fluid), and a few M⊕ of hydrogen–helium (about 15% of the mass).

The 11.2-hour rotation gives a rotation parameter q = ω²R³/GM ≈ 0.08 and a flattening of ~0.05 (using the Uranus/Neptune ratio of flattening to q). This is clearly oblate, with equatorial radius ~1,800 km larger than the polar radius. At 35 K and 10.3 m/s² the atmospheric scale height is ~12 km.

The heat budget is the reason Mot can be this cold. A 1.2 M♃ hydrogen giant of the system's ~2 Gyr age would still radiate ~240 times more heat than it absorbs, and would sit near 130 K. An ice giant with a stratified interior and a rock core of ~4 M⊕ is different. Radiogenic heating of that core at 4–10 pW/kg gives (1–2.4)×10¹⁴ W (0.007–0.016 W/m²), comparable to the ~0.016 W/m² needed. The rest leaks out of the layered interior as primordial heat.

Mot has no solid surface at the cloud level. "Surface" on this page means the top of the stably stratified ice-haze deck (~0.1–1 bar). The planet has no well-developed ring system. Scattered, faint dusty arcs and diffuse bands lie inside the fluid Roche distance (~2 R, ~70,000 km); they are fed by minor-moon disruption and impact debris. Plausible but not directly attested.

## Atmosphere and composition

The atmosphere is mostly molecular hydrogen and helium, with methane enriched deep down, as in Uranus and Neptune. The cold changes the behaviour of every minor constituent.

**Methane.** Saturation pressure falls steeply with temperature, from 0.1 bar at 90 K to 3×10⁻⁶ bar at 50 K and 10⁻¹⁰ bar at 35 K. Anchoring a dry adiabat at 35 K near 0.2 bar, methane at a ~3% mixing ratio condenses at ~6–7 bar and ~95 K (approximate, abundance-dependent). Above that deck methane gas is cold-trapped to mixing ratios below ~10⁻⁴ at 1 bar and far lower higher up. The visible clouds are therefore not a methane deck. They are tenuous ices and photochemical hazes sitting high in the stratified layer.

**Hydrogen and helium do not condense.** H₂ has a critical temperature of 33.2 K, so no liquid hydrogen or hydrogen "glaciers" form at these pressures. Seasonal collapse is of nitrogen and the heavier trace species.

**Nitrogen and seasonal haze.** At 31.8 K the N₂ saturation pressure falls to ~2.5×10⁻⁷ bar, so the trace nitrogen condenses from the upper atmosphere as microcrystalline ice haze near apastron and sublimes again toward periastron, when it absorbs latent heat rather than releasing it. These ice-haze fallouts last decades to centuries. Fine crystalline N₂ and CH₄ ice that sparkles in dim starlight is the "diamond dust" of Mot, as in Earth's polar clear-sky ice crystal precipitation.

**Haze and albedo.** The low albedo (0.11, adopted) comes from dark, cosmic-ray- and UV-processed hydrocarbon and nitrile haze, similar to the dark material on many outer-system bodies. It is a material property, not a consequence of weak illumination.

**Vertical structure.** Temperatures are lowest, ~30–35 K, near the top of the stratified deck and rise with depth to ~95 K at the methane base (~6–7 bar). Condensation near the top and weak mixing below give thin, discontinuous layers. Thermal inversions occur where hazes absorb starlight, but starlight is so weak that these are minor.

**Slow weather.** The radiative response time at 35 K is ~75 years at 0.2 bar and ~380 years at 1 bar (vs. a 792-year orbit). Seasons therefore lag the forcing by a large part of a season, and weather systems and vortices persist for decades to centuries. This "fossil weather" is real slow dynamics, not frozen material. Cloud bands drift at glacial speeds relative to the 11.2-hour rotation.

## Interior and heat

Mot is a differentiated, layered body: a rock–iron core, a very deep mantle of water, ammonia, and methane ices, and a thin H₂–He envelope. The mantle's composition changes with depth enough to keep it stably stratified, like the layering inferred for Uranus. That limits convection and explains why the interior stays warm but releases little heat. Layers are separated by diffuse boundaries rather than sharp interfaces.

Radiogenic heat from the core and slow leakage of primordial heat supply ~2.3×10¹⁴ W. Tidal heating from the small moon family is negligible. The heat does not escape uniformly. Where stratification is thinnest or double-diffusive layering breaks down, heat concentrates and can drive stronger plumes. This patchy output is the origin of the heat-driven storms in the next section.

A thin shell of ionic, electrically conducting water–ammonia at a large fraction of the radius supports a weak, strongly non-dipolar magnetic field (Uranus/Neptune-like), with a strength of tenths of a gauss at the cloud tops. The field's off-centre, tilted geometry gives asymmetric polar magnetospheric structure. Deep in the planet carbon from methane may be compressed into diamond at very high pressure, but this is deep interior and has no surface expression.

## Megastorms and heat release

Weak internal heat and condensable methane interact in a way that is directly relevant to Mot. In a hydrogen-rich atmosphere with abundant heavy condensables (CH₄ here), moist convection is inhibited because the condensing gas makes the air column heavier. Heat then builds up beneath a stable layer until it is released in a rare, vigorous overturn. This is the known mechanism proposed for episodic storms on Uranus and Neptune. At Mot the same mechanism, with a far lower heat flux, would produce infrequent, planet-scale storms that recur over centuries.

These **megastorms** are the planet's cryovolcanic analogue. A storm erupts from a deep "dynamic eruption zone", lofts methane ice and warm gas tens of kilometres up, and can punch through the cold haze to expose warmer layers (brightness temperatures up to ~95 K, the ~6–7 bar level, in storm-cleared windows). They are visible in the infrared and ultraviolet. Plausible extension, not directly attested at 117 AU.

- **Crystalline debris fields.** Ice particles falling out after a storm accrete into drifting fractal-like haze sheets and crystal-fan structures in the cloud deck, sculpted by slow winds.
- **Thermal scars.** Warm ovals and streaks of warmed gas, persisting for decades to centuries as the vortex slowly drifts.
- **Pressure-wave rings.** Concentric, circular ripples in the haze decks (atmospheric gravity and Rossby waves launched by the storm), spanning thousands of kilometres and sometimes overlapping into interference patterns without any impactor.

The storms also recycle volatiles. Ejected ice contributes to upper-atmosphere haze, and expelled volatiles return to equilibrium by condensation and photochemical processing over megayear timescales, so spectral features shift slowly over time.

## Polar regions

Polar night lasts many decades to centuries. With no starlight, the upper troposphere settles toward the ~23 K floor set by internal heat alone, in practice ~24–30 K once heat transported from lower latitudes is included. This is cold enough to condense N₂, CH₄, C₂H₂, C₂H₆, and HCN hazes, and to make a thick polar haze cap.

- **Stratified haze towers.** Vertical stacking of ice-haze layers sorted by condensation temperature and particle size, with the heaviest and least volatile species deepest. Over millions of years these sorted layers form the "element towers": coherent, stratified columns of haze and cloud tens to hundreds of km tall. Silanes and phosphine are not carried because silicon and phosphorus stay in the deep rock core.
- **Hexagonal and moiré patterns.** Polar jets and vortices on rapidly rotating planets organise into polygonal waves (Saturn's north polar hexagon is the known example). On Mot, several polygonal wave modes and ice-haze banding overlap, producing hexagonal cells and moiré interference patterns covering hundreds to thousands of kilometres. They drift slowly because the radiative response time is so long.
- **Aurora and luminescence.** Auroral emission is very faint (no solar wind to speak of at 117 AU). It comes from trapped energetic particles and galactic cosmic rays striking H₂ at the magnetic poles, in the deep red to near-ultraviolet, and can persist for weeks in one place. Cosmic-ray-irradiated ice haze may store energy and release it as faint luminescence when storm upwelling warms it; this is speculative and not established.
- **Magnetic structure.** The weak, asymmetric field concentrates charged-particle precipitation and low-frequency plasma and radio emission near the poles. Radio bursts are weak and brief; radio telescopes at Valen have detected brief low-frequency pulses that correlate with deep-winter auroral activity or post-eruption atmospheric collapse. The "ice circuits" and "electrodynamic spires" are best read as magnetically organised ion and haze structures, not solid crystal filaments. Speculative.

## Moons and satellites

Mot has a sparse but dynamically stable satellite system: one dominant moon and a family of small irregular bodies.

**Mot I** carries over 99.5% of the satellite mass. It orbits retrograde at ~580,000 km (~17 R), in a near-circular orbit with period 9.3 days. Its radius is ~1,200 km, smaller than both Triton (1,353 km) and Europa (1,561 km). At ~1.9 g/cm³ it has a mass of ~1.4×10²² kg (about 8×10⁻⁵ of Mot). The retrograde, inclined orbit points to capture of a minor planet from the system's outer debris field. Tidal circularisation by dissipation inside Mot I takes ~0.5 Gyr for Q/k₂ = 10³ and ~5 Gyr for 10⁴, so a near-circular orbit after 2 Gyr is plausible.

Spectroscopy suggests a surface of water ice mixed with ammonia hydrates over a possibly differentiated interior. Radiogenic heat is ~3×10¹⁰ W (~2×10⁻³ W/m²) and sunlight is only 0.31 W/m², far too little for Triton-style geysers. Linear fractures and signs of slow cryovolcanic resurfacing are therefore weak, with no plume activity confirmed. Mot I is not the source of the megastorms.

**Minor moons.** At least 11 irregular, dark, low-albedo bodies 2–40 km across, with chaotic inclinations and eccentricities. They are captured remnants from the end of planet formation or fragments of past collisions. They show no geological activity and are probably primitive ice–rock mixtures, known mostly from occultations and faint thermal signals. Efforts to name Mot I formally are pending among research groups on Valen and Aialon.

## Physical basis

**Classification.** A 1.2 M♃ hydrogen giant of about 2 Gyr would radiate about 1.3×10¹⁸ W (roughly 240 times the ~5.5×10¹⁵ W it absorbs at 117 AU) and sit near 120–140 K, so a 35-K body cannot be one. Mot is a Neptune-class **frozen ice giant** of about 30 M⊕ and 34,000 km radius (1.09 g/cm³, 10.3 m/s², 26.5 km/s escape, q = 0.080, flattening ≈0.05, scale height 12 km at 35 K), with about 3–5 M⊕ of rock, about 20 M⊕ of ices and a few M⊕ of H₂–He; its Hill radius is 2.66 AU (2.42 at periastron) and its period 792.5 yr (1,238 Valen-years).

**Heat.** Mot absorbs 0.0688 W/m² (T_eq 33.2 K, A = 0.11) and radiates an internal 0.016 W/m² (2.3×10¹⁴ W), giving T_eff = 35.0 K (36.4 K at periastron, 33.8 K at apastron). A 4 M⊕ rock core at 4–10 pW/kg supplies 0.007–0.016 W/m², and the rest leaks from the stratified interior. The polar-night floor from internal heat alone is 23 K.

**Atmosphere.** CH₄ saturation is 0.106 bar at 90 K, 3×10⁻⁶ at 50 K and 1.2×10⁻¹⁰ at 35 K; a 35-K, 0.2-bar anchored adiabat puts a 3% methane cloud base near 6.5 bar and 95 K. N₂ saturation is 2.5×10⁻⁷ bar at 31.8 K, so trace nitrogen condenses near apastron, and H₂ (critical 33.2 K) does not condense. The radiative time is 76 yr at 0.2 bar and 378 yr at 1 bar (c_p = 1.2×10⁴ J/kg/K), which gives seasonal lag and long-lived vortices. Megastorms are the cryovolcanic analogue (condensation-inhibited moist convection releasing stored heat; plausible extension, not directly attested at 117 AU), hexagons are polygonal wave modes and the stratified "towers" are sorted haze columns.

**Mot I.** 1,200 km radius, about 1.9 g/cm³, 1.4×10²² kg (8×10⁻⁵ of Mot) at 580,000 km (17 R), period 9.3 d, retrograde; fluid Roche distance ≈2 R; eccentricity damping 0.5 Gyr (Q/k₂ = 10³) to 5 Gyr (10⁴). Radiogenic heat of 3×10¹⁰ W (2×10⁻³ W/m²) and 0.31 W/m² of sunlight are too little for Triton-style geysers.

**Limits.** The 0.016 W/m² internal flux, rock fraction, radiogenic rate, albedo, c_p and the adiabat anchor are adopted or rough; no interior or radiative-transfer model is run, and the storm mechanism, luminescence and magnetic structures are speculative.

Mot is a low-contrast world to the human eye, with dim grey-blue haze bands and little visible motion. In infrared, ultraviolet, and magnetic imagery it comes alive.

The poles are covered by vast hexagonal and moiré ice-haze patterns across thousands of kilometres, alternating between opaque and translucent crystal haze, with deep violet shadows and periodic aurora flashes. Tall stratified haze columns rise like frozen antennae.

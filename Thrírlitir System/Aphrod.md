**Aphrod** is a mildly warm, ocean-covered circumbinary planet in the [[Thrírlitir System]], orbiting the G+M binary pair [[Thrírlitir]] B and Thrírlitir C at a distance of 1.0 AU. It receives about 0.61 times Earth’s insolation from the B+C pair, plus an additional ~0.05 Earth flux from the distant F-type primary Thrírlitir A, for a total stellar flux of roughly 0.66 F🜨. A dense nitrogen–ammonia–methane atmosphere and extensive cloud cover maintain a mean surface temperature near 280 K despite the relatively low incident light. Numbers are mostly scaling arithmetic; see the physical basis at the end. Once considered an uninhabitable “Venus analog”, it has surprised observers with its temperate ocean and living biosphere.

With a mass of 1.7 M🜨 and a radius of 1.15 R🜨, Aphrod is a slightly larger and denser terrestrial world (ρ ≈ 6.1 g/cm³) with surface gravity about 1.3 times Earth’s. Around 85% of the surface is covered by deep ammonia–water oceans buffered at high pH, overlying iron-rich crustal rocks. The combination of cold upper atmosphere, warm lower troposphere, and alkaline oceans supports a chemically complex biosphere, including iron-based microbial mats and an aerial ecosystem of methane-oxidizing and phototrophic organisms.

## Orbital and Physical Characteristics

Aphrod orbits the common barycenter of Thrírlitir B and Thrírlitir C at 1.0 AU, in a nearly circular, low-inclination circumbinary orbit. The central binary has a combined luminosity of 0.6065 L☉, giving Aphrod a baseline insolation of ~0.61 F🜨 from B and C. The more distant primary star Thrírlitir A, at ~7 AU from the B+C pair, contributes an additional ~0.05 F🜨 on average, so Aphrod experiences a total radiative input of about 0.66 times that of Earth.

The mixed G+M illumination leads to a dual-band optical environment. At 1 AU the mean photon flux at the top of the atmosphere is about 535 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in 600–700 nm and 1,385 in 800–1,100 nm. Thrírlitir B supplies most of the photons in both windows (88% and 86%), A adds about 8% and 6%, and the M-type star C adds only about 4.5% and 7%, so the near-infrared band is mostly B's red tail with a small extra shoulder from C. Aphrod’s dominant phototrophs are tuned to these two windows, with pigment systems analogous to ammonia-modified tetrapyrroles. The 53.7-day binary cycle makes the B+C flux swing between 0.54 and 0.72 F🜨 (0.59–0.77 with A) every 64 days (the binary–planet beat), damped by the dense atmosphere and ocean. At 1.37 times the binary's circumbinary critical radius (0.730 AU), the orbit sits well outside the unstable zone, near the 6:1 resonance with the binary's period, and was stable in all N-body screens.

With 1.7 Earth masses and 1.15 Earth radii, Aphrod’s mean density (6.10 g/cm³) implies a rocky, iron-enriched interior. Surface gravity is ~1.3 g🜨, high enough to support a thick atmosphere (>10 bar) while still allowing vigorous convection and cloud dynamics. Rotation is slow but non-synchronous with the circumbinary orbit, producing long days and complex tides driven by the three suns and the planet’s oceanic mass distribution.

## Atmosphere and Climate

Aphrod’s atmosphere is dominated by nitrogen (N₂), with significant amounts of ammonia (NH₃), methane (CH₄), and carbon dioxide (CO₂), plus trace hydrogen cyanide (HCN) and formamide (HCONH₂). Surface pressures are 10–20 bar, and the troposphere supports a strong greenhouse effect, lifting the mean surface temperature to ~280 K above an effective radiating temperature near 230 K. Holding 280 K from 0.66 Earth flux needs a gray optical depth of about 1.6 at a Bond albedo of 0.30 (1.4 at 0.25, 2.1 at 0.40), which collision-induced N₂ absorption, CH₄, NH₃ and CO₂ at 10–20 bar can plausibly supply; a radiative-transfer calculation has not been done. Ammonia is the sensitive component: the NH₃ vapor above a 15–25 wt% ocean is about 0.3–0.6 bar at 260 K, 0.7–1.2 bar at 280 K and 1.4–2.3 bar at 300 K (a 35 wt% ocean would hold 1.7 bar at 280 K and 3.3 bar at 300 K), rising 1.9 times per 20 K, so the ocean's ammonia content acts as a greenhouse thermostat and was set to 15–25 wt% to keep it manageable.

High-altitude haze and deep cloud decks give Aphrod a bright, veiled appearance in visible light, reminiscent of a “mild Venus” rather than a frozen world. Vertical temperature structure is strongly stratified: the upper atmosphere is cold and photochemically active, while the lower troposphere and near-surface layers are warm, convective, and chemically rich.

Photochemistry in the upper atmosphere slowly converts CH₄ and NH₃ into nitriles, haze particles, and complex organics, which sediment into the lower layers. There, they are reprocessed by redox-active microbes and abiotic catalysts, forming part of a vertical organic cycle between ocean and sky.

## Surface and Oceans

Aphrod’s surface is dominated by a global ocean of mixed water and ammonia (H₂O–NH₃), with 15–25% NH₃ by mass in the bulk ocean and somewhat lower near the surface due to ongoing gas exchange. These oceans are cold by terrestrial standards but thermodynamically mild for an ammonia world, with typical surface temperatures between 275 and 295 K under a dense, humid atmosphere.

The ocean is strongly alkaline (pH ~10.0–11.2; an unbuffered ammonia solution would sit near pH 12), buffered by dissolved ammonia, carbamates, and carbonate species. It can absorb tens of bars of CO₂ as ammonium carbonate and carbamate (about 56–94 bar for a 3-km-deep ocean at 15–25 wt% NH₃), so atmospheric CO₂ stays small. Shallow regions over iron-bearing crust expose minerals such as goethite (FeO(OH)) and ferrihydrite (Fe(OH)₃), which act as redox-active substrates for both abiotic chemistry and metabolism.

Volcanism and geothermal activity are generally weak to moderate, supplying CO₂, trace H₂, and reduced metals. These inputs help sustain Aphrod’s iron–methane–nitrogen cycles and maintain a steady background of prebiotic feedstock molecules, including formamide and HCN, in the oceans and lower atmosphere.

## Redox cycles and energy budget

The biosphere's iron–methane and nitrogen cycles are kept, and they are powered by light. The balanced overall reactions, with Fe(II) and Fe(III) held in alkaline solids, are:

- **Photoferrotrophy** (light-driven recharge of the oxidant): CO₂ + 4 Fe(OH)₂ + 3 H₂O → CH₂O + 4 Fe(OH)₃. It needs about 4 photons of about 1.3 eV per carbon fixed, roughly 23% efficient, so the dual-band light is more than enough: even 1% of the photons reaching the cloud decks could in principle fix hundreds of grams of carbon per square metre per year.
- **Iron–methane oxidation** (methanotrophy with Fe(III) minerals): CH₄ + 8 Fe(OH)₃ → CO₂ + 8 Fe(OH)₂ + 6 H₂O. It yields about −73 to −95 kJ per mole of methane between pH 7 and 11 (roughly 1–2 ATP, assumed electrode potentials), a slow-growing biosphere's energy.
- **Ammonia oxidation by iron** (ammoniferrites): 2 NH₃ + 6 Fe(OH)₃ → N₂ + 6 Fe(OH)₂ + 6 H₂O. Nitrogen returns from N₂ to NH₃ by biological and abiotic fixation (hydrothermal N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃ on iron minerals).

Above pH 10, producing dissolved Fe²⁺ is thermodynamically unfavorable (about +67 kJ per mole of methane at pH 10 for 1 μM Fe²⁺), so Fe(II) circulates as solids (green rust, siderite, Fe(OH)₂) and as ligand complexes (cyanide, ammine, carboxylate), moving by sedimentation, spray and aerosols. Manganese acts as a higher-potential oxidant; its regeneration is a speculative branch.

Photolysis of CH₄ and NH₃ in the upper atmosphere lets hydrogen escape (about 10⁹–10¹⁰ H cm⁻² s⁻¹, Titan-like, photolysis-limited). Over 1 Gyr that oxidizes 3.5×10²⁰–3.5×10²¹ mol of iron (2×10¹⁹–2×10²⁰ kg, 0.6–6% of the crustal iron) and could convert up to about 10% of the ocean's ammonia to N₂, building the planet-wide Fe(III) reserve slowly; the photoferrotrophs run the cycles from day to day.

## Biosphere Overview

Aphrod is home to a mature, metal-driven biosphere built around iron-, nitrogen-, and carbon-redox chemistry in its alkaline oceans and lower atmosphere. The dominant life forms are microbial, organized into surface mats, pelagic communities, and cloud biota. Larger macroscopic organisms primarily inhabit the troposphere, forming filters, grazers, and drifters that feed on suspended microbes.

Main article: [[Life on Aphrod]]

Surface microbial communities are organized into guilds adapted to specific redox niches and substrates. Idealized guilds inherited from early metabolic evolution include:

- **Ferrimethanotrophs** – oxidize methane using Fe³⁺ as a terminal electron acceptor, producing CO₂ and Fe²⁺.
- **Ammoniferrites** – oxidize NH₃ while reducing Fe³⁺, generating N₂ and Fe²⁺.
- **Methylcyanogens** – transform HCN into nitriles and oligomers, capturing energy from HCN activation and oxidation.
- **Carbamotrophs** – use ammonium carbamate (NH₂COONH₄) as a combined C/N source, producing small organics and N₂.
- **Porphyridials** – deploy ammonia-modified tetrapyrroles for CO₂ fixation and partial NH₃ oxidation, acting as the closest analogs to photosynthetic primary producers.
- **Manganopiles** – oxidize NH₃ via radical intermediates (NH₂•, N•) coupled to Mn(IV)/Mn(II) cycling, driving high-potential nitrogen turnover.
- **Formamidogens** – oxidize or disproportionate formamide, releasing CO/NH₃ and feeding Fe³⁺ reduction or substrate-level phosphorylation.

Iron- and nitrile-rich particles, organic micelles, and droplets act as both nutrient packages and physical scaffolds for airborne microbial communities.

## Physical basis

**Light.** At 1 AU the mean-flux photon fluxes at the top of the atmosphere are about 535 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ in 600–700 nm and 1,385 in 800–1,100 nm. Thrírlitir B supplies 88% and 86% of those photons, A about 8% and 6%, and C only 4.5% and 7%, so both windows are mostly B's red tail, with a small extra shoulder from C. Mean total flux is 0.66 Earth flux (B+C 0.535–0.721 over the binary phase plus A 0.051); the planet sits 1.37 times the circumbinary critical radius out and near the binary's 6:1 resonance (0.99 AU), with a 64.2-day binary–planet beat.

**Climate and ammonia.** Holding 280 K at 0.664 Earth flux needs a gray optical depth of 1.4 (albedo 0.25), 1.6 (0.30), 2.1 (0.40) or 2.8 (0.50), plausible for 10–20 bar of N₂, CH₄, NH₃ and CO₂ (not computed with radiative transfer). The adopted albedo is about 0.30–0.35. Ammonia vapor above the ocean (pure-NH₃ Antoine vapor pressure, activity coefficient 0.85) is 0.23, 0.49 and 0.94 bar at 260, 280 and 300 K for 10 wt%, 0.34/0.73/1.41 for 15 wt%, 0.45/0.97/1.87 for 20 wt%, 0.56/1.21/2.33 for 25 wt% and 0.79/1.68/3.25 for 35 wt%; it rises 1.9 times per 20 K, so the ocean's ammonia content is a greenhouse thermostat and is adopted at **15–25 wt% NH₃** (0.7–1.2 bar of vapor at 280 K). An unbuffered ammonia solution is about pH 12; carbonate and carbamate buffering holds the ocean at pH 10–11.2. A 3-km-deep ocean at 15–25 wt% holds 1.4–2.4×10²² mol of NH₃ and can absorb about 56–94 bar of CO₂ as ammonium carbonate, so atmospheric CO₂ stays small.

**Redox cycles.** The balanced overall reactions use Fe(II) and Fe(III) in alkaline solids: photoferrotrophy CO₂ + 4 Fe(OH)₂ + 3 H₂O → CH₂O + 4 Fe(OH)₃ (about 4 photons of 1.3 eV per carbon, ΔG ≈ +114 kJ/mol, about 23% efficient); iron–methane oxidation CH₄ + 8 Fe(OH)₃ → CO₂ + 8 Fe(OH)₂ + 6 H₂O (about −73 to −95 kJ per mole of methane between pH 7 and 11, assumed electrode potentials, about 1–2 ATP); and ammonia oxidation 2 NH₃ + 6 Fe(OH)₃ → N₂ + 6 Fe(OH)₂ + 6 H₂O, with nitrogen returned by biological and abiotic fixation. Above pH 10 producing dissolved Fe²⁺ is endergonic (about +67 kJ/mol CH₄ at pH 10 for 1 μM Fe²⁺), so Fe(II) circulates as solids (green rust, siderite, Fe(OH)₂) and ligand complexes (cyanide, ammine, carboxylate). The engine is **photoferrotrophic recharge plus hydrogen escape**: hydrogen escape of 10⁹–10¹⁰ cm⁻² s⁻¹ (Titan-like, photolysis-limited) oxidizes 3.5×10²⁰–3.5×10²¹ mol of iron per Gyr (2×10¹⁹–2×10²⁰ kg, 0.6–6% of the crustal iron) and could convert up to about 10% of the ocean's ammonia to N₂, building the Fe(III) reserve slowly while photoferrotrophs run the daily cycle. The manganese branch (Mn(IV) regeneration) is a speculative branch.

**Buoyancy.** Air density is 1.4 kg/m³ (1 bar, 230 K) to 5.6 (5 bar, 280 K). An NH₃-filled bladder lifts 0.9–1.9 kg/m³ and an H₂-filled one 1.3–5.1; a 1-m-radius bladder carries a 0.4 kg/m² skin and a 10-m one 4 kg/m² (NH₃). Cells of 1, 10 and 50 μm radius settle at 0.2 mm/s, 2 cm/s and 0.6 m/s and stay aloft in convective updrafts of 0.1–1 m/s only if they are small or carry vesicles. The cloud habitat is the lower troposphere and deep cloud deck at about 3–5 bar and 265–280 K.

**Limits.** The greenhouse requirement, NH₃ activity, electrode potentials, hydrogen-escape fluxes and crustal iron are assumed or rough; no radiative transfer, ocean chemistry or photochemistry is run, and the ocean ammonia content, the Mn recharging branch and the iron-complex shuttle remain to be verified.

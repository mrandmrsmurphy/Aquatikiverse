**Ceraun** is a close-in gas giant orbiting the F-type primary star [[Thrírlitir#Thrírlitir A|Thrírlitir A]] in the [[Thrírlitir System]]. With a mass of about 150 Earth masses (~0.47 M♃) and a radius slightly larger than Jupiter’s, Ceraun is a strongly inflated hot Jupiter. It orbits at 0.1 AU with a period of roughly 10.1 days and is tidally locked, presenting the same hemisphere to its star at all times.

At this distance Ceraun receives about 250 times the stellar flux that Earth receives from the Sun. The resulting equilibrium temperature is around 1080 K under uniform redistribution and Bond albedo 0.1, with the dayside likely reaching 1300–1500 K while the nightside cools to below ~800 K. These extremes drive supersonic jet streams, extensive chemical disequilibrium, and continuous atmospheric escape that feeds a helium-rich, partially ionized tail, mostly anti-stellar and swept back about 15° along the orbit, glowing in ultraviolet, hydrogen-alpha and sodium emission for telescopes. Numbers are mostly scaling arithmetic; see the physical basis at the end.

Though not a candidate for habitability, Ceraun is an important laboratory for understanding the climates and magnetospheres of highly irradiated gas giants.

## Orbital and Physical Characteristics

Ceraun follows a nearly circular, low-inclination orbit around [[Thrírlitir#Thrírlitir A|Thrírlitir A]] at a semi-major axis of 0.1 AU. Keplerian dynamics around the 1.3 M☉ host star give an orbital period of about 10.1 days. At this proximity, the combined gravitational and tidal forces have synchronized Ceraun’s rotation with its orbit, locking one hemisphere into perpetual starlight and the other into permanent night with respect to A.

The planet’s mass of ~150 M🜨 and radius of ~1.1 R♃ correspond to a bulk density of ~0.44 g/cm³, significantly lower than that of Jupiter. This low density indicates a large, heated envelope of hydrogen and helium with internal inflation driven by intense irradiation and residual formation heat. Surface gravity at the visible “cloud top” level is ~9.7 m/s², comparable to Earth’s despite the much larger size, and the escape velocity is about 39 km/s.

Ceraun’s orbit lies well within the stable S-type orbital zone around the primary star and far interior to the orbit of the B+C binary pair. The combined luminosity of [[Thrírlitir#Thrírlitir B|Thrírlitir B]] and [[Thrírlitir#Thrírlitir C|Thrírlitir C]] adds far below 0.1% to the total flux at Ceraun’s location under the described hierarchy; their main effect is to provide secondary, shifting points of light in the planet’s sky during conjunctions, a bright pair that lights its “night” side with about 0.0124 Earth flux (about 1,240 lux) whenever it is above the horizon, thermally negligible against the 250-times-Earth dayside flux.

No regular moons are known or expected, as the strong tidal field from Thrírlitir A and the planet’s proximity make long-lived satellites unlikely. Instead, Ceraun may host transient ring material or captured debris within its Roche-dominated region. The planet's synchronous-orbit radius (about 1.05 million km) lies outside its Hill sphere (about 728,000 km), so any moon spirals inward; its Roche lobe reaches 485,000 km (6.2 planetary radii), the planet fills 16% of it, and tidal locking takes only about 7 Myr, far less than the system's age.

## Atmosphere and Climate

Ceraun’s atmosphere is composed primarily of molecular hydrogen (H₂) and helium (He), with a rich admixture of high-temperature species including water vapor (H₂O), carbon monoxide (CO), sodium (Na), potassium (K), and refractory absorbers such as titanium oxide (TiO) and vanadium oxide (VO). Trace methane (CH₄) may persist on the nightside and in deeper, cooler layers.

The intense stellar flux and low albedo (~0.1) heat the dayside upper atmosphere to temperatures above 1300 K. Under these conditions, sodium and potassium remain partially vaporized, imprinting strong resonance lines in the optical spectrum. TiO and VO act as strong absorbers at visible wavelengths, creating a thermal inversion in the dayside stratosphere: temperatures increase with altitude over part of the atmospheric column.

A characteristic scale height of ~500 km reflects both the high temperature and the moderate surface gravity. At the terminator region probed during transits, temperatures vary rapidly with altitude and longitude, producing a chemically complex and spectrally rich limb.

Because Ceraun is tidally locked, the substellar point experiences continuous irradiation, while the antistellar hemisphere never sees direct light from A, although B and C can illuminate it. The resulting temperature gradient drives:

- **Supersonic equatorial jets** that carry heat from the dayside to the nightside.
- **High-altitude day–night winds** that can exceed several km/s.
- **Cooler return flows** at greater depth, where radiative cooling is slower and temperature contrasts are reduced.

Despite these circulation patterns, heat redistribution is incomplete. The nightside remains hundreds of kelvin cooler than the dayside, allowing partial condensation of species such as H₂O and, at depth, silicate clouds. This leads to strong chemical disequilibrium between the hemispheres and the terminator regions.

The dayside is dark and hot: a Bond albedo of about 0.1 with absorbing TiO and VO, a thermal inversion and dayside temperatures of about 1,280–1,380 K for dayside redistribution or none (up to about 1,530 K at the substellar point; 1,080 K under uniform redistribution). Silicate and iron clouds condense at about 1,700–1,900 K, deeper than the dayside photosphere, and TiO and VO are cold-trapped on the cool nightside, so the bright yellow-white cloud tops, with darker belts and vortices, appear on the cooler limb, at high latitudes and across the nightside, fading to deep red and an infrared glow near the hot dayside. In thermal emission the dayside dominates, with phase curves revealing the offset of the hottest region eastward of the substellar point due to the superrotating jet.

## Magnetosphere and Atmospheric Escape

Ceraun likely contains a deep layer of metallic hydrogen that supports a substantial planetary magnetic field. Observations and models suggest a magnetic dipole strongly tilted (~70°) relative to the rotation axis. This geometry produces complex auroral ovals that sweep across both the day and night hemispheres over the course of an orbit.

The combination of a strong magnetosphere, intense stellar wind from the F-type host, and high upper-atmosphere temperatures drives continuous atmospheric escape. Ultraviolet irradiation and electron impacts populate a long-lived metastable helium state (He⁰ 2³S), which is efficient at absorbing near-infrared photons around 1083 nm.

As a result, Ceraun exhibits:

- A **hydrodynamic outflow** of hydrogen and helium from the dayside exosphere.
- An extended, partially ionized **helium tail**, mostly antisolarward for many planetary radii and swept back from that direction by 10–36° (about 15° for a 400 km/s wind) because the planet moves at 107 km/s through the wind, so "antisolarward" and "trailing along the orbit" describe the same tail. It glows in ultraviolet, hydrogen-alpha and sodium emission for telescopes; the 1083-nm helium line is seen in absorption, not emission.
- **Asymmetric transit signatures** in helium 1083 nm and Lyman-α, with deeper absorption on the leading ingress or trailing egress depending on wind structure.
- Intense **auroral emissions** in UV and radio bands where magnetospheric currents couple with the upper atmosphere.

The energy-limited loss at 10% efficiency is about 8.7×10⁶ kg/s, driven by about 3.4 W/m² of extreme ultraviolet and X-rays (some 740 times Earth's), or 0.046 Earth masses per Gyr (0.06% of the planet's mass over the system's 2 Gyr): not enough to strip the planet’s bulk envelope, but it significantly shapes the structure of the upper atmosphere and the near-planet plasma environment. For a 2–10 G surface dipole and a wind ram pressure near 75 nPa at 0.1 AU the magnetopause stands off at 9.7–16.6 planetary radii (0.76–1.3 million km), beyond the Roche lobe.

## Observation and Scientific Interest

From the perspective of observers on [[Aialon]] and [[Valen]], Ceraun is one of the brightest and most accessible targets in the Thrírlitir System for high-precision spectroscopic study:

- As a close-in, transiting hot Jupiter, it produces large and frequent **transit signatures**, enabling detailed transmission spectroscopy of its limb.
- **Secondary eclipses** and full-orbit phase curves in infrared wavelengths constrain the day–night temperature contrast and the efficiency of heat transport.
- The strong **helium 1083 nm absorption** lines provide direct diagnostics of atmospheric escape rates and wind speeds in the upper atmosphere.
- From Aialon, Ceraun transits A every 10.29 days for about 4.9 hours, a 0.70% dip as a dark 2.4′ dot crossing the 27.3′ disk. It never strays more than 3.6° from A (0.8° from Valen), so it is lost in A's glare there; its reflected light is about magnitude −10 from Valen against A's −23, and about −13 from Aialon, where it may appear briefly near A at dusk and dawn.
- Radio and UV monitoring can probe the interaction between Ceraun’s magnetosphere and the stellar wind, offering a nearby testbed for theories of star–planet magnetic coupling.

Ceraun serves as a benchmark for comparative planetary climatology within the Aquatikiverse: a natural experiment in what happens to a hydrogen-rich giant planet baked by an F-type star, with a tilted magnetosphere, strong winds, and a visible plasma tail, all embedded within a dynamically rich trinary stellar system.

## Physical basis

**Bulk and orbit.** 150 M⊕ at 1.1 R_J gives 0.44 g/cm³, 9.67 m/s² at the cloud tops and a 39 km/s escape speed; the period is 10.13 days and the orbital speed 107.4 km/s. Tidal locking takes about 7 Myr (Q = 10⁵, 10-hour initial spin). The Roche lobe is 485,000 km (6.2 planetary radii; the planet fills 16% of it), the Hill radius 728,000 km, and the synchronous-orbit radius (1.05 million km) lies outside the Hill sphere, so no moon survives.

**Temperature and clouds.** With T = 1,108 K × [4f(1 − A)]^¼ (f = ¼ uniform, ½ dayside, ⅔ none, 1 substellar), a Bond albedo of 0.1 gives 1,079, 1,283, 1,379 and 1,526 K. The dayside is dark, hot and absorptive (Bond albedo about 0.1, TiO and VO, a thermal inversion), with silicate and iron clouds condensing at about 1,700–1,900 K below the dayside photosphere and bright cloud tops on the cooler limb, at high latitudes and across the nightside. B+C light the planet with 0.0124 Earth flux (about 1,240 lux), 0.005% of the dayside flux.

**Escape and tail.** An XUV flux of about 3.4 W/m² (about 740 times Earth's; assumed Lx/Lbol about 10⁻⁵) gives an energy-limited loss of 8.7×10⁷ kg/s at 100% efficiency and **8.7×10⁶ kg/s at the adopted 10%** (0.046 M⊕/Gyr, 0.03% of the planet's mass per Gyr, 0.06% over 2 Gyr). The 107 km/s orbital motion through a 150–600 km/s wind sweeps the tail 10–36° (15° at 400 km/s) back from the anti-stellar direction, so the helium-rich tail is one structure, mostly anti-stellar and bent along the orbit; it glows in ultraviolet, hydrogen-alpha and sodium emission for telescopes (the 1083-nm helium line is absorption). For a 2–10 G dipole and about 75 nPa of wind ram pressure the magnetopause stands off at 9.7–16.6 planetary radii, beyond the Roche lobe.

**Sky.** From Aialon Ceraun transits A every 10.29 days (0.70% depth, about 4.9 hours, a 2.4′ dot on a 27.3′ star) and never strays more than 3.6° from A; from Valen it is within 0.8° of A. Its reflected light is about magnitude −10 from Valen against A's −23 (about −13 from Aialon, where it may appear near A at dusk and dawn), so it is lost in A's glare except in transit.

**Limits.** Temperatures, escape, tail geometry, Roche and Hill scales, tidal locking and transit geometry are scaling arithmetic with an assumed extreme-ultraviolet flux (Lx/Lbol about 10⁻⁵), escape efficiency, wind speed and dipole strength, not a circulation, photochemistry or magnetohydrodynamic model; the cloud and TiO/VO picture is qualitative.

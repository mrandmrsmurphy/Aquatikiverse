**Barzél** is the metallic dwarf planet circling the G-type star [[Thrírlitir#Thrírlitir B|Thrírlitir B]] at 0.05 AU. It is a dense iron–nickel body baked by 226 times Earth's sunlight, the hottest dwarf planet in the [[Thrírlitir System]]. Its sibling [[Pekhám]] circles Thrírlitir C. Both are close-in dwarf planets in S-type orbits in a tight binary.

## Orbit and stability

The Holman–Wiegert critical radius for a planet orbiting B is 0.101 AU (binary separation 0.3 AU, e = 0.01), so Barzél at 0.05 AU sits at about half the limit. N-body screens of A, B, C, Barzél and Pekhám (10⁵ years) keep Barzél stable out to 0.10 AU and lose it at 0.12 AU. The orbit must stay within roughly 30° of the B–C orbital plane; at 50° it is destabilised in ~5×10⁴ years. Thrírlitir C does not "counterbalance" the orbit into stability. Barzél is stable because it lies deep inside B's own gravitational domain. C raises a small forced eccentricity (~0.01–0.02), and the 4.43-day period is 1/12.1 of the binary period, not at a low-order resonance.

From Barzél's sky B is a 9.4° disc, about 18 times the Sun's width from Earth. C is a 0.8° orange disc that rises and sets every 4.43 days and brightens the sky to 0.3–0.7 F⊕, and A is a 0.1° point. [[Pekhám]] is a dark ~11″ speck at best.

## Light and heat

Barzél is tidally locked to B within ~10⁴ years. The ">1,000 K" figure is the reference for a uniformly redistributed, zero-albedo body (1,080 K; 988 K at albedo 0.3). The actual surface is strongly two-faced because there is no atmosphere to move heat.

- **Hot face.** The substellar point reaches ~1,530 K for A = 0 (~1,470 K for albedo 0.15), and the dayside hemisphere averages ~1,280 K (~1,230 K).
- **Liquid-metal temperatures across the dayside.** Lead (melting 600 K, boiling 2,022 K) stays liquid over essentially the whole lit hemisphere, because 600 K is reached within ~2° of the terminator. Iron–nickel stays solid, since 1,811 K is never reached. The Fe–FeS eutectic (~1,260 K) is exceeded in a large region around the substellar point, so sulfide melts form there (FeS itself melts at ~1,460 K, reached only near the substellar point).
- **Zinc.** Zinc boils at 1,180 K, below the temperature of most of the hot face (which is above 1,180 K within ~65° of the substellar point), so zinc cannot sit as liquid lakes there. It vaporises, flows to the cooler side, and condenses as frost across the night hemisphere. The night hemisphere is the zinc reservoir, with slow net loss from the hot side. Plausible, not computed.
- **Night hemisphere.** C passes overhead every 4.43 days and heats the ground to ~320 K at its brightest, then it falls toward 30–50 K in the dark half. Zinc and sulfur-bearing frosts sit there stably. Poles receive only grazing light from B and C (both lie in the orbital plane), so polar craters are the coldest ground.
- **Internal heat.** Tidal heating from the forced eccentricity is ≲0.01 W/m², and an iron-rich body carries little radiogenic heat. Both are negligible against the ~3×10⁵ W/m² of incident starlight at the substellar point.

## Surface and composition

Barzél's 7.8 g/cm³ is about that of uncompressed iron: its centre pressure is only ~13 GPa. It is therefore a nearly pure iron–nickel body, perhaps with a sulfide fraction and a thin crust of low-melting metals, resembling an exposed metal core.

- **Dayside.** Solid Fe–Ni alloy plains, with lead (and tin) lakes across the lit hemisphere and sulfide melts around the substellar point.
- **Hot-face vapor.** Zinc and sulfur vapor, with traces of lead, make a thin, hot exosphere that streams toward the night side. Stellar X-ray/UV (0.31 W/m² from B, at assumed Lx/Lbol ~10⁻⁶) would strip a thick atmosphere at ~2% of the planet's mass per Gyr at 10% efficiency, so the vapor is a surface-supported exosphere, not a lasting atmosphere.
- **Cold hemisphere.** Zinc frost and sulfur-bearing frosts coat the night side, re-evaporating at each dawn.

## Activity

Heat from within is too weak for volcanism, so surface activity is thermally driven. The ground swings through ~300 K every 4.43-day orbit across the night hemisphere and through the full hot-face gradient across the terminator, so repeated expansion and contraction fracture the metal plain. Fissures glow in the infrared where they expose hot rock below. Sulfide pools bubble along the substellar region and vent sulfurous gas, and zinc and sulfur vapor jet from fractures at the terminator, where frost meets the hot face. Seen in the infrared, Barzél glows like a forge or a live ember, brightest along fissures and in the sulfide basins of the substellar region.

**Valen** is a partially terraformed terrestrial planet orbiting the G+M binary pair [[Thrírlitir]] B and Thrírlitir C in a circumbinary orbit at 0.80 AU. Slightly larger and heavier than Earth, Valen receives roughly Earth's mean flux from two suns whose 53.7-day mutual orbit modulates the planet's illumination by about ±18% (every 69.7 days as seen by the planet), with a distant bright F star, Thrírlitir A, adding about 5%. The world has a 23-hour day, a 20° axial tilt and a surface gravity of about 1.1 g⊕. Numbers are mostly scaling arithmetic; see the physical basis at the end.

Before human intervention, Valen possessed liquid-water oceans and a dense nitrogen–carbon dioxide atmosphere but was apparently sterile. Terraforming campaigns from [[Aialon]] are about 70% complete in converting its 79% world ocean and twin supercontinents into a living biosphere. Oxygen has risen to about 0.15 bar and carbon dioxide has been drawn down to about 0.003 bar; an ozone layer is forming, but large parts of the surface remain biologically empty and ultraviolet radiation is still hazardous away from domes and seeded regions.

## Orbital and stellar context

Valen orbits the common barycenter of Thrírlitir B (0.85 M☉, G-type) and Thrírlitir C (0.4 M☉, M-type) at a semimajor axis of 0.80 AU. The planet sits at 1.10 times the circumbinary critical radius (0.730 AU) and well away from the binary's 4:1 resonance at 0.757 AU, where the planet would be unstable; in a long N-body screen at 0.80 AU it stays stable (see the stage page). Keplerian dynamics around the 1.25 M☉ central mass give an orbital period of about 0.640 years (233.8 days), 4.35 times the binary's period. The B+C pair contributes about 0.95 Earth flux on average (0.82–1.18 over the binary phase) and A about 0.05 at 7 AU, for a mean of about 1.0 F⊕.

The B+C binary has a separation of 0.3 AU and completes an orbit every 53.7 days. As the stars move around their barycenter the flux at Valen oscillates, and the planet's motion makes the pattern recur every 69.7 days (the binary–planet synodic period): when both suns are on the near side the flux peaks, and when C swings behind B it dips. The flux also follows the binary-driven distortion of the planet's own orbit, whose instantaneous eccentricity ranges up to about 0.06–0.2, so the 10-day-averaged total flux typically spans 0.82–1.48 F⊕ with a mean of 1.03–1.10. Oceanic heat capacity and atmospheric inertia smooth these variations, but short "binary seasons" are superimposed on the 0.640-year year.

Valen and [[Aphrod]] share the B+C subsystem. Their 0.80-AU and 1.0-AU orbits give a period ratio of about 1.398, within 0.15% of 7:5, with no exact resonance asserted; they approach within about 0.05–0.15 AU at times and stay stable in the screens. Valen's year is 0.640 years; the 53.7-day binary cycle and the 11.60-year stellar cycle are the named cycles of its calendar, and the 69.7-day beat and the Aialon–Valen conjunction every 1.012 years are kept for record-keeping.

## Physical characteristics

Valen's mass is about 1.33 times Earth's, with a mean radius of 1.10 R⊕. The bulk density of about 5.5 g/cm³ indicates an iron–nickel core and silicate mantle broadly like Earth's. Surface gravity at mean radius is about 10.8 m/s² (~1.1 g⊕) and the escape velocity is about 12.3 km/s.

The sidereal rotation period is 23 hours (a mean solar day of 23.09 hours). An axial tilt of 20° produces moderately strong seasons, more pronounced than Aialon's tilt-driven ones. The tilt is rock steady: spin precession is about 158 arcsec per year (40 from the stars, 117 from the moon pair), while the orbital plane precesses around the binary axis every 27.9 years (12.9° per year), which wobbles the pole by only about ±1°.

With a surface area of about 617 million km² and a volume of about 1.44×10¹² km³, Valen offers somewhat more land than Earth, most of it currently ocean. The Hill radius of about 1.22 million km defines a generous gravitational sphere of influence for moons and near-planet spacecraft.

## Moons and tides

Valen hosts a distinctive binary-moon system: two major satellites orbiting a common barycenter that in turn orbits Valen at 250,000 km (21% of the planet's Hill radius) in a near-equatorial orbit with a period of about 12.5 days.

- **Valen I** is the larger and rockier body, about 0.6 lunar masses (4.4×10²² kg) with a radius of about 1,470 km.
- **Valen II** is smaller and more ice-rich, about 0.3 lunar masses (2.2×10²² kg) with a radius of about 1,380 km (density about 2.0 g/cm³).

The two are 12,000 km apart and circle each other every 1.44 days. That is 34% of the pair's mutual Hill radius (35,100 km; stable below about half of it) and well outside the Roche limit between them (about 4,250 km). Seen from Valen they are two Moon-sized discs up to 2.75° apart. This configuration produces:

- **Complex tidal patterns:** an equilibrium lunar tide of about 1.3 m from the pair (nearly 3.6 times Earth's lunar tide) plus about 0.43 m from the two stars, about 1.7 m in all, with beat frequencies and varying tidal range as the moons align and oppose each other.
- **Obliquity stabilization** far stronger than Luna provides for Earth (see above).
- Distinctive **double and overlapping eclipses**, which are culturally prominent among Valen's colonists.

Several much smaller irregular satellites and captured debris also reside within the Hill sphere but are dynamically and visually minor.

## Atmosphere and climate

### Pre-terraforming state

Before terraforming, Valen's atmosphere had a surface pressure of about 0.9 bar and was dominated by nitrogen (partial pressure ~0.8 bar, about 89%) and carbon dioxide (~0.1 bar, about 11%), with trace noble gases and minor species but essentially no molecular oxygen. Despite the high CO₂, the average surface temperature was only about 270 K because of a very high planetary albedo (bright clouds and unweathered continental surfaces, with CO₂ condensing at high latitudes) and a modest water-vapor greenhouse over cool oceans. The lack of ozone and of biological UV screens made the surface chemically active but hostile to nascent life; photochemistry produced small amounts of carbon monoxide, nitrates and peroxides but did not lead to abiogenesis within the planet's recorded geologic history.

### Current terraforming stage

- **Total surface pressure** is about 0.95 bar, reached through controlled volatile delivery and gas recycling.
- **Oxygen partial pressure** has risen from zero to about 0.15 bar (about 16% by volume), driven by industrial splitters and engineered photosynthetic biospheres, which is like living at about 2,700 m on Earth and calls for acclimatization.
- **Carbon dioxide** has been drawn down by mineral carbonation reactors and ocean fertilization from 0.1 bar to about 0.003 bar (about 8 times Earth's present level), because at Valen's flux CO₂ is not needed for warmth and a high CO₂ level would make the planet too hot; it is also safe for people at that level.
- **Nitrogen** remains the primary constituent at about 0.78 bar (about 82% by volume), supplemented by imported nitrogen and trace gases.

An ozone layer is forming in response to rising O₂, particularly over regions of dense primary productivity, but the global ozone column is still patchy and thinner than Earth's, allowing high levels of ultraviolet radiation at many latitudes. The stars themselves are not UV-rich (the photospheric UV-B reaching Valen is about 0.7 of Earth's, since B's UV output is weaker than the Sun's and C's is small), so the hazard comes from the thin ozone; the sun-screening measures are needed outside seeded biomes and settlements.

The present global mean temperature is about 290 K, with equatorial oceans often exceeding 295 K and polar regions retaining seasonal sea ice. The 20° axial tilt and Earth-like flux yield familiar temperate, tropical and polar belts, but the 69.7-day flux modulation introduces additional variability:

- Short-period "binary seasons" influence cloud cover, storm strength and surface heating on timescales felt by ecosystems and agriculture.
- Double-sun geometries create complex daily light curves, with days featuring two shadows, staggered sunrises (up to about 1.4 hours apart) or brief partial eclipses.

Prevailing circulation includes robust Hadley cells, strong mid-latitude jet streams and large storm systems in the mid-latitude ocean belts between the two supercontinents.

## Surface and hydrosphere

Valen's surface is dominated by a global ocean covering about 79% of the planet. The remaining 21% forms two major supercontinents separated by the world ocean and roughly antipodal: one lies primarily in the northern hemisphere with broad equatorial peninsulas, the other spans more southern latitudes with a large inland seaway and extensive continental shelves.

This configuration produces strong **ocean currents** that transport heat between hemispheres and smooth the flux oscillations, extensive coastal margins and shallow seas ideal for early colonization and biosphere expansion, and interior continental regions that remain dry and in many cases biologically barren. With the tides of about 1.7 m, the coasts are strongly tidal.

Hydrospheric engineering has adjusted salinity and nutrient inventories toward Earth-like values, though some basins preserve pre-terraforming chemistries. Ice caps are currently modest and seasonally variable, indicating a climate near the middle of Valen's habitable range.

## Pre-terraforming history and sterility

Despite long-term orbital and climatic stability, Valen appears to have remained abiotic prior to human arrival. Several hypotheses are under study:

- **Early sterilizing impacts** during the system's heavy bombardment phase may have repeatedly reset nascent prebiotic chemistry.
- The initial volatile inventory may have been **CO₂-heavy but organics-poor**, limiting pathways to stable polymers.
- The circumbinary UV environment may have favored destructive photolysis over the accumulation of complex molecules at key epochs.

By the time sophisticated surveys from Aialon examined Valen, the planet exhibited stable oceans, weathered continents and an oxidizable crust but no detectable biosignatures beyond trace photochemical organics. That made it an attractive candidate for ethical terraforming: habitable by physics but apparently unoccupied by indigenous life.

## Terraforming and colonization

Terraforming of Valen follows, but does not duplicate, the earlier Aialon program. About 70% of it is complete (oxygen has reached 0.15 of a 0.21 bar target); at an Aialon-scale effort of 6.4×10¹⁴ W the oxygen built so far took about 700 years (1.4×10²⁵ J) and the remaining 0.06 bar needs about 280 years (5.7×10²⁴ J), roughly 300 years more. Key phases include:

- **Atmospheric adjustment:** controlled CO₂ drawdown (from 0.1 to about 0.003 bar) via mineral carbonation reactors and ocean fertilization, combined with imported nitrogen and trace gases to tune pressure and climate. Producing 0.15 bar of O₂ (3.0×10¹⁹ mol) needs more reduced carbon than the original CO₂ held (2.0×10¹⁹ mol), so at least 0.09 bar-equivalent of extra carbon (2.1×10¹⁷ kg) came from carbonates, ocean inorganic carbon and delivered organics.
- **Microbial seeding:** introduction of engineered extremotolerant microbes tailored to the double-sun photoperiod and the enhanced UV exposure of the thin ozone layer, designed to fix carbon, cycle nitrogen and weather rock.
- **Hydrological modulation:** construction of reservoirs, managed wetlands and ice-control schemes to minimize runaway glaciation or desertification on the supercontinents.
- **Macroscopic life introduction:** gradual release of plants, fungi and animals derived from Aialon biota but modified for higher gravity, more frequent short-term light variations and incomplete ozone shielding.

Colonization has begun from a small number of domed coastal cities established during the early atmospheric stages; they serve as hubs for terraforming operations, logistics and scientific research, and shelter their people from the hazardous ultraviolet. Much of the planet's land remains visually pristine, with only scattered microbial carpets and experimental test biomes.

## Developing biosphere

Valen's biosphere is in an early successional stage compared with Aialon's mature ecology:

- **Primary producers** include engineered plankton and hardy pioneer plants in coastal regions, tuned to exploit both the G-type and M-type stellar spectra.
- **Soil formation** is actively underway on previously sterile regolith, driven by microbial weathering, lichen-like consortia and mechanical terraforming techniques.
- **Faunal introductions** are tightly controlled and geographically limited, focusing on small, rapidly reproducing species that can test ecological stability without causing global disruptions.

Microbial life is confined to introduced communities and their dispersal zones, including seeded coastal and plankton waters; the remaining oceans, thin abiotic substrates and bare rock are still sterile. Large contiguous wilderness regions have been deliberately left unseeded as scientific reference zones and for philosophical reasons, preserving examples of a "physics-habitable but lifeless" world.

## Sky and illumination

Valen's sky is one of its most distinctive features. To surface observers:

- The **primary sun** (Thrírlitir B) is a 0.59° disc, somewhat smaller and whiter than the Sun as seen from Earth.
- The **secondary** (Thrírlitir C) is a 0.31° deeper orange-red disc that brightens and dims over its 53.7-day dance around B, never more than 22° from it.
- Days feature **double shadows**, shifting relative brightness as the stars diverge or converge, and occasional transits or partial eclipses when one star passes in front of the other.
- The distant primary **Thrírlitir A** is a small 0.10° disc that supplies about 5% of the light (about 5,100 lux): for about half of each year it hangs in the night sky and nights are as bright as a heavy overcast day.

These patterns, together with the frequent apparitions of [[Aphrod]] and the telescopic transits of [[Ceraun]] across the distant star A every 10 days (Ceraun itself is lost in A's glare), give Valen's sky a complex, dynamic character that strongly shapes Valenic art and timekeeping.

## Cultural and scientific significance

Valen is viewed within Valenic civilization as the "younger twin" of [[Aialon]]: less finished, more dangerous and more open to experiment. It functions as a laboratory for large-scale ecological engineering under a circumbinary sun and a testbed for long-term coexistence between engineered biospheres and still-sterile regions.

Philosophical debates over how quickly and how completely to seed Valen have become central to interplanetary politics, particularly regarding the status of uncolonized supercontinental interiors and untouched polar basins.

## Physical basis

**Orbit and flux.** Valen's semimajor axis is 0.80 AU (period 0.640 years, 233.8 days; 4.35 times the binary's period). B+C give 0.95 Earth flux on average (0.82–1.18 over the 53.7-day binary phase, recurring every 69.7 days as seen by the planet) and A about 0.05; with the binary-driven distortion of the orbit (instantaneous eccentricity up to about 0.06–0.2) the 10-day-averaged total spans about 0.82–1.48 Earth flux with a mean of 1.03–1.10. The mean solar day is 23.09 hours for a 23-hour sidereal day.

**Sky.** B is a 0.59° disc, C a 0.31° disc up to 22° from B (sunrises staggered by up to about 1.4 hours) and A a 0.10° disc supplying about 5,100 lux, so for about half of each year nights are as bright as a heavy overcast day. Partial mutual eclipses of B and C are possible because the planet's inclination offset (about 0.26°) is below the sum of the stars' angular radii (0.44°).

**Ultraviolet.** Per unit flux, B's photospheric UV-B is 0.70 of the Sun's, C's 0.07 and A's 1.38; the UV-B reaching Valen is about 0.69 of Earth's before ozone (C's chromospheric UV excluded). The hazard is the thin ozone layer, not UV-rich stars.

**Climate and air.** With a mean flux near 1.016 and an albedo of 0.30, an Earth-like greenhouse gives about 289 K, and 0.04 bar of CO₂ would add roughly 20–25 K (about 309 K). Holding about 288 K at 0.03–0.05 bar of CO₂ would need an albedo near 0.5, and the pre-terraform 270 K at 0.1 bar of CO₂ needs an albedo near 0.6–0.67 (rough). The adopted state is low CO₂ with an albedo of about 0.37: CO₂ ≈0.003 bar (about 8 times Earth's), mean ≈290 K, N₂ ≈0.78 bar, O₂ ≈0.15 bar (about 16%, like 2,700 m on Earth), Ar ≈0.01 bar, total ≈0.95 bar. Outdoor CO₂ is then safe for people; ultraviolet exposure and the thin oxygen are the hazards.

**Terraforming.** One bar of Valen's air is 5.71×10¹⁸ kg (1.97×10²⁰ mol). The O₂ built so far (0.15 bar, 2.96×10¹⁹ mol) took 1.4×10²⁵ J (about 700 years at Aialon's 6.4×10¹⁴ W); the remaining 0.06 bar to reach 0.21 needs 5.7×10²⁴ J (about 280 years), so the work is about 70% done with roughly 300 years left. The original CO₂ (0.1 bar, 1.97×10¹⁹ mol) cannot supply 0.15 bar of O₂ by reduction alone, so at least 0.09 bar-equivalent (2.1×10¹⁷ kg) of extra carbon came from carbonates, ocean inorganic carbon and delivered organics.

**Moons, tides and tilt.** Two major moons orbit their barycenter at 250,000 km (21% of Valen's 1.22-million-km Hill radius; 12.5 days): Valen I, 0.6 lunar masses (rocky, radius about 1,470 km) and Valen II, 0.3 lunar masses (ice-rich, about 1,380 km). They are 12,000 km apart with a 1.44-day mutual period and up to 2.75° apart in the sky; the separation is 34% of the pair's 35,100-km mutual Hill radius (stable below about half) and well outside the 4,250-km Roche limit between them. Equilibrium tides are about 1.3 m from the pair and 0.43 m from the stars (about 1.7 m, 3.4 times Earth's). Spin precession is about 158 arcsec/yr (40 from the stars, 117 from the pair) while the orbit plane precesses around the binary axis every 27.9 years (12.9° per year), so the 20° tilt stays steady to about ±1°.

**Cycles.** Named cycles are Valen's year (0.640 years), the 53.7-day binary cycle and the 11.60-year stellar cycle; the 69.7-day beat and the Aialon–Valen conjunction every 1.012 years are record-keeping.

**Limits.** Flux and eccentricity numbers come from the N-body screen (10-day samples, random phases); the CO₂ greenhouse estimate uses logarithmic forcing (5.35 ln(C/C₀)) with a sensitivity of 0.8 K per W/m² and an Earth baseline of 33 K; ozone and terraforming figures are scalings with assumed efficiencies, not a climate or photochemistry model (the 270 K pre-terraform state implies a very high albedo of about 0.6 by a rough greenhouse estimate); the moon pair is not integrated, and its geometry, tides and tilt stability are Hill-radius, equilibrium-tide and precession-constant estimates.

**Life on Jaspis** refers to the diverse array of solid-state, carbon-based organisms inhabiting the exoplanet [[Jaspis]] in the [[Lytherin System]]. These organisms exhibit a radically alien biology: they use mechanical strain as a major energy store alongside chemical and electronic free energy, move by releasing structural tension, and grow through atmospheric polymerization of carbon compounds into extended nanostructures.

Unlike Earth life, which relies on liquid water and cellular metabolism, Jaspian organisms operate entirely in the solid state. Their movement and rapid energy storage are mechanical, their “tissues” are carbon lattices, and their replication carries heritable information through templated surface chemistry rather than DNA or RNA. Biochemically foreign yet internally coherent, the Jaspian biosphere presents one of the most compelling known examples of non-aqueous, non-cellular life.

## Biochemical Basis
The Jaspian biosphere is rooted in [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/carbon)–[nitrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/nitrogen) lattice chemistry, shaped by the planet’s hot surface and locally extreme volcanic conditions and thick atmosphere—rich in carbon monoxide (CO), acetylene (C₂H₂), and hydrogen cyanide (HCN). Rather than relying on water or any fluid solvent, Jaspian organisms function entirely in solid or semi-solid phases, where life processes occur through surface chemistry and polymer growth.

Organisms assemble and maintain their bodies using an array of carbon-based nanostructures, including:

* Graphene ribbons and planar sheets
* Twisted bundles of carbon nanotubes
* Carbyne filaments (linear carbon chains)
* Polymeric nitrile lattices formed from atmospheric HCN

Membrane analogues, where present, are composed of fullerenes or hexagonal carbon–nitride composites that compartmentalize surface reactions. Some species go further, producing external shells of silicon carbide, titanium carbide, or layered graphene armor—granting protection and thermal buffering in volatile environments.

Metabolic exchange and replication occur via surface-bound catalytic sites, where new material is accreted from atmospheric reactants. Without an internal liquid solvent, life on Jaspis is more geological than biological in appearance—its boundaries often indistinct from terrain, its growth patterns slow but persistent.

## Core Life Processes
### Energy Capture and Storage
The organisms derive energy primarily from sharp thermal gradients—such as those between day and night, or in geothermal and volcanic zones. Maintained thermal gradients, photon capture and redox reactions supply usable work. Organisms store some of that work as elastic strain in nanomaterials; the springs do not supply primary energy, and their behavior still depends on interatomic bonding. There is no Earthlike ATP-based carrier system.

* **Storage:** Energy is retained by twisting nanotubes, compressing carbon lattices, or straining polymeric frameworks. These materials function like biological springs.
* **Release:** Organisms rapidly discharge stored energy to power sudden movements, break apart carbon structures, or deploy defensive mechanisms—such as snapping shells or firing projectile spines.
* **Alternative Sources:** Some autotrophs harvest energy directly via ultraviolet or near-infrared light, using electron transfer in carbon semiconductors. Others exploit atmospheric redox gradients, particularly those involving CO and acetylene.

### Growth and Recycling
Growth occurs by polymerizing airborne carbon species—primarily CO, C₂H₂, and HCN—onto existing lattice structures. Specialized catalytic tips or surface-bound enzyme analogues guide and template this assembly.

* **Raw Materials:** Common feedstocks include carbon monoxide, acetylene, nitrile aerosols, and hydrocarbon dust.
* **Catalysis:** Surface-bound nanostructures initiate and direct polymer formation, often incorporating elements from decaying organisms.
* **Recyclers:** Detritivores play a vital role by scavenging carbon debris—such as soot, fragmented nanotubes, or fullerene shards—and repurposing them into new structural material.

### Reproduction
Jaspian organisms reproduce through physical templating that propagates inherited growth motifs, rather than a DNA/RNA code. Copying errors and selectable variations allow Darwinian evolution. Most species reproduce by fission, budding, or direct lattice extension from parent structures. There is no confirmed analog to DNA or RNA; instead, inheritance likely occurs through templated propagation of physical growth motifs.

* **Shelled Species:** These organisms expand beneath existing exoshells, periodically shedding outer layers once internal growth reaches a critical threshold.
* **Swarming Events:** In volatile environments, reproductive surges sometimes occur as synchronized waves of budding, lattice detachment, or motile juvenile dispersal—especially during sudden thermal influxes.

## Ecological Roles and Taxa
Jaspian life exhibits a range of ecological strategies shaped by the planet’s extreme conditions. Organismal categories are approximate and reflect mechanical function rather than genetic taxonomy.

### Autotrophs
Sessile or creeping entities that assemble biomass from atmospheric carbon compounds using redox work, light or heat flow across a sustained hot–cold gradient.

* Tend to inhabit geothermal rifts, fracture zones, and irradiated plains.
* Slow-growing, forming dense mat-like colonies or crystalline structures.

### Detritivores
Carbon recyclers that decompose broken structures and atmospheric soot.

* Often immobile, living in dunes or sedimentary deposits.
* Key to lattice turnover and ecosystem maintenance.

### Predators
Mobile planimals that strike using stored mechanical energy.

* Capable of spring-loaded attacks and ballistic motion.
* Range from coiled lattice-stalkers to jointed, crab-like ambush organisms.

### Aquatic Species
Inhabit viscous cool sheltered basins of mixed heavy aromatic/nitrile liquids or subsurface nitrile tar pools.

* Locomotion by flexure or tension-assisted jets.
* Low metabolism; high chemical selectivity in nutrient uptake.

### “Turtles”
Robust, heavily armored forms specialized for defense in high-risk zones.

* Collapse flat against terrain when threatened.
* Some have multi-layered reflective shells with embedded semiconductors for slow energy harvesting.

## Evolution and Origins
Abiogenesis on Jaspis likely proceeded via thermochemical templating and surface-catalyzed polymerization, rather than in aqueous environments. Complex carbon frameworks may have emerged initially from soot deposits or graphite faults under ultraviolet flux. The biosphere is estimated to be ~400 million years old, based on sedimentary carbon isotope layering and atmospheric equilibrium models.

The development of complex tension-based locomotion and hard-shell defense likely reflects intense predatory competition and volatile surface conditions.

## Intelligence and Culture
No intelligent or tool-using species have been identified. No confirmed sapient lineage is known; its mechanical architecture is not a demonstrated barrier to intelligence. While some social behavior (e.g., synchronized lattice growth or collective shell shedding) has been hypothesized, there is no evidence of language, symbolic interaction, or structures made by the native planimals. This does not deny the independently established manufacture of the broader stellar system.

## Notes

* Life on Jaspis challenges standard biochemical assumptions by replacing aqueous solvents and chemical ATP analogs with solid-state mechanics and carbon lattice engineering.
* The planet is considered a high-priority case study for xenobiologists and astromechanical theorists.

## Sensation, habitats and supplementary forms

Planimal thermal sensors use deformable nanostructure arrays; seismic and vibrational receptors detect substrate motion. Rift autotrophs grow toward maintained heat-flow interfaces and use springs sparingly. Carbon-dune detritivores may remain almost immobile. Plains support rolling, jumping and spring-pouncing forms; predators harvest both preloaded strain and chemically usable matter. Diamond uplands favor slow growth and multilayer armor, while dangerous volcanic terrain favors robust turtles and opportunists. Turtle shells provide strong finite protection, not literal indestructibility.

Cool polar refugia, including sites above roughly 75° latitude where local phases permit it, can contain mixed heavy-aromatic fluids; anthracene and phenanthrene are candidate mixture components rather than proof of a pure-liquid sea. Sheltered subsurface nitrile-rich polymeric tar pools provide additional external fluids for creeping, burrowing, flexure-swimming and tension-jet forms. Mixture melting, boiling, polymerization and thermal decomposition must be checked at each habitat temperature. They replace the incompatible pure acetylene lakes and benzene rivers.

## Physical basis

Photons, redox work and maintained hot–cold gradients supply the planimals' energy, and springs store it; recycled carbon is material, not a perpetual energy source. Templates carry heritable information and selectable variation without DNA or RNA.

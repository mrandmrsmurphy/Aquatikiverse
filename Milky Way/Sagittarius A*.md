# Sagittarius A*

## Real astronomical background

**Sagittarius A***, abbreviated **Sgr A***, is the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way. It is also identified through its compact radio emission. Stellar motions and observations of surrounding emitting gas establish its nature. The black hole itself emits no escaping light from inside its event horizon.

Viewed from Earth, the source lies in Sagittarius at approximately **17h 45m 40.04s, −29°00′28.1″** (J2000/ICRS). The Event Horizon Telescope released its first image in 2022, using observations made in 2017. The bright radio structure surrounds a dark central region; it is not an ordinary visible-light photograph.[^wiki]

The GRAVITY Collaboration’s 2022 multiple-star fit gives a mass of **(4.297 ± 0.012 ± 0.040) × 10⁶ M☉** and distance **8,277 ± 9 ± 33 pc**, with statistical and systematic uncertainties separated. These are a particular published measurement reference, not a claim that all later analyses give identical parameters.[^gravity]

The EHT imaging analysis finds a roughly **50-microarcsecond emission ring**, consistent with a black hole of about four million solar masses at roughly 8 kpc. Emission-ring size, black-hole shadow, and event-horizon size are different quantities.[^eht]

Sgr A* is faint relative to its Eddington luminosity. Its quiescent bolometric luminosity is of order **10²⁹ W**, while its quiescent X-ray luminosity is much smaller; infrared and X-ray flares vary strongly. An increase in one observing band is not necessarily the same fractional increase in bolometric power, visible illumination, or charged-particle dose.[^flares]

## Aquatikiverse connection

[[Ghulheim and Ferruva]] places the fictional brown dwarf–moon pair about **0.05 pc** from Sgr A*. Neither that pair nor its biosphere is an observed discovery. The fictional system uses the Galactic Center direction and about 8.3 kpc Earth distance; the original Monoceros/2,000-pc metadata is superseded. Its adopted model is on [[Ghulheim and Ferruva]].

Using 4.3 × 10⁶ M☉ as a rounded point-mass reference, a 0.05-pc Kepler orbit has a period of approximately **505 years**. This is a calculation for the fictional setting, not an observed orbit. The setting uses an illustrative eccentricity of 0.05 (pericenter about 0.0475 pc); its delivery history, surrounding stellar potential, encounters, and orbital evolution require additional modeling.

# Middle Path

![[images/Middle_Path_its_tethered_moon.png|700]]

*Middle Path and its Tethered Moon: a representative illustration, not to scale, whose moon proportions and surface patterns do not match the adopted dome.*

**Middle Path**, designated **Mirar's Veil c / AQ-MV-01 c**, is a tidally locked terrestrial planet of the [[Ɬiʔa System]], orbiting the active red dwarf [[Mirar's Veil]]. One face looks at the star for ever, so the star hangs fixed in the sky and the stars wheel once for every orbit. A very light hemispherical dome, the **Tethered Moon**, hangs at the star–planet Lagrange point above the substellar point and throws a permanent shadow, the **Stillpoint**, at the planet's heart. Around that shaded basin lie a calm dry eye, a pinned storm wall, a hot bright ring and, beyond them, a broad belt of temperate land where humans live under a star that never moves. A dense, noble-gas-rich, electrically restless world with two independent biospheres, it is the setting where a native silicon biosphere shares a planet with transplanted Earth life.

All numbers on this page are adopted values. Most are scaling arithmetic, not simulation; see the physical basis at the end.

The 1.0-g surface gravity and a substantial iron core fix the mass and radius together: at 1 g the radius follows R = √M in Earth units, and 1.05 M⊕ gives the largest core compatible with it. A 1.5 M⊕ planet at 1 g would need a density below that of iron-free rock.

## Orbit, rotation and the sky

Middle Path completes one rotation per orbit, so the star stays at one place in the sky. Its position shifts only by the libration of a slightly eccentric orbit (±1.15° of east–west swing, ±2% flux each c-day). The star is 1.76° (104′) across, 3.3 times the Sun's disk, with a luminosity of 0.005904 L☉, a 3200 K spectrum (17.7% visible, 82.3% infrared) and a roughly 2-day spin that gives it strong fields and flares.

**Position and time.** A place on the dayside is fixed by its star's elevation and bearing: the star's elevation equals 90° minus the distance from the Stillpoint, and the star's azimuth points toward the Stillpoint. The stars turn once per c-day (0.86° per hour), a slow clock. There are no days: all time is the planetary calendar, built on the neighboring planets' exact 3:2 chain (periods 2/3, 1, 3/2, 9/4 and 27/8 c-days for Two-Face, Middle Path, Crying Eye, Epilepticus and Belted King).

The other cycles (6/5, 9/5 and 27/19 c-days from the alignments, 1.09 c-days for the Corpse, 1.0117 c-days and an 86.25 c-day year for the Shield Couple) and the short clocks of about an eighth of a c-day (the Shield Couple's 2.116-day mutual orbit and the star field's 2.260-day polarity flip) are kept for record-keeping.

**Neighbors.** Two-Face and Crying Eye are resolved discs 17–18′ across at closest (0.55 times the Moon seen from Earth), Epilepticus and Belted King 5–6′ and the Corpse 1–1.5′. Two-Face never strays more than 49.7° from the star and transits it, a little over 3% of its disk, every 2 c-days; the outer planets circle the whole sky once per synodic cycle and pass behind the star. The **Shield Couple** is a naked-eye twin of stars (Sapphire magnitude about 3.6–3.9, Rust 3.3–3.5, combined about 2.8) up to 2.4′ apart, splitting and closing every 2.1 days; a 10 cm telescope resolves their 28″ discs, Sapphire's polar hexagon and storms. In the star-locked sky they pass behind the star for about 2 hours each c-day.

**The Tethered Moon in the sky.** The moon appears directly **below** the star's center, 109′ across (3.5 times the Moon's size): it nibbles the star's lower limb inside about 60° from the Stillpoint (star elevation 30°), and from about 80° outward (star elevation 10° or less) hangs just detached beneath the star, a dark disk between the star and the horizon. The tether is invisible.

## The Tethered Moon

An enormous, ancient, extraordinarily light artificial structure holds the planet's climate in a delicate shape. It is a **hemispherical dome**: the curved side faces the planet, so inhabitants never see its flat profile, and the flat face points at the star as a sunshade. It is held by automatic active control near the star–planet L1 point on the line through the substellar point and the magnetic pole, and is joined to the ground by an active tether.

The moon intercepts about 23% of the starlight that would reach the planet; because the star's disk is nearly as wide as the dome, the penumbra, not the umbra, sets this cost (even a dome with no umbra would need a radius of 2,909 km). Seen from the ground within 88° of the Stillpoint it is a plain dark disk, its underside lit only by planetshine (about 0.2 W/m²). At the Stillpoint it is a total eclipse, with the star's corona as an aureole and flares at its edge.

**Control.** L1 is unstable (e-folding time 1.10 days), so the dome holds station by active control. Sunlight pressure on the flat face (1.4×10⁸ N absorbing to 2.8×10⁸ N reflecting) is balanced by the star's excess pull beyond L1, so the equilibrium sits 9–18 km sunward of L1; changing the face's reflectivity moves it by about 9 km, which is the steering authority, and the steady stellar-wind load shifts it by about 0.6 km. With an orbital eccentricity of about 0.01 the substellar point librates ±131 km, the dome swings ±3,840 km sideways, the tether leans ±1.15° and changes length by ±1,987 km each c-day; the anchor stays inside the umbra. The tether carries a specific potential of about 6.1×10⁷ J/kg from the ground to L1, tapering 4.6 times (a 4×10⁷ J/kg material) to 1.8 times (10⁸ J/kg). The moon is declared immune to magnetospheric effects and sits about five times farther out than the magnetopause.

**Origin.** The builders and purpose are unknown; the structure predates the present civilization and lies far beyond its capability. Traditions speak of emissaries ascending the tether to a great room where it meets the dome and of an ancient message or presence there; these remain traditions, not a confirmed discovery.

## Interior and magnetosphere

**Core and heat.** The core of light-element-rich Fe–Ni (about 15 wt% S, C and Si, with minor Cu and Ti) carries 0.5 wt% potassium, 0.5 ppm uranium and 2 ppm thorium: about 209 TW of heat today, ≈26 TW of dynamo power. The planet as a whole is potassium-rich (40–60 times Earth's potassium), with a surface heat flow of 0.6–0.75 W/m², hot, volcanic and tectonically vigorous. Electrical conductivity does not set the field strength, so exotic metals contribute only through buoyancy and heat.

**Field.** A slow 17.4-day rotation would make an ordinary dynamo multipolar; two declared premises make it dipolar and strong. The fixed tidal bulge selects the dipole axis along the star direction, so the magnetic pole sits at the substellar point, and a chiral additive (TaAs-like inclusions) in the outer core drives the dynamo to its ohmic limit. The surface field is about **144 μT** at the equator (4.6 times Earth's), about 288 μT at the poles, and was about 160 μT 1 Gyr ago. Roughly 144 μT is the energy-limited maximum: sustaining 500 μT would need 312 TW of ohmic dissipation.

**Magnetopause.** Planet c is sub-Alfvénic (Alfvén Mach 0.54), so there is no bow shock; the total pressure is the stellar ram pressure (303 nPa) plus the star's ambient magnetic pressure (521 nPa).

**Star–planet coupling and aurora.** The star's field sweeps past at about 370 km/s, a motional electric field of 0.42 V/m and a potential drop of about 32 MV across the magnetosphere, with 180–1,770 TW of coupling power (efficiency 0.1–1) that is comparable to the planet's own heat flow, and polarity flips every 2.26 days. The planet is dramatically auroral: the oval sits about 24° from the Stillpoint when quiet, over the bright ring and the eyewall, sweeps out to 37° in storms and 47° in flares, and puts about 5.8 W/m² of upper-atmosphere heating into the oval (about four times the saturated XUV flux) and about 0.9 TW of radio. The compass needle points at the Stillpoint everywhere on the dayside. Ground fields from field swings are large: 14 V/km for a 5 μT swing in ten minutes and 144 V/km for a 50 μT swing in a hundred-times storm (10⁻³ S/m ground), with 0.5–4 V/km even on quiet days.

**A recent change.** Until about 10 Myr ago the star rotated about every 50 days, slower than the planet orbits, so the planet outran the star's field (relative speed ≈31 km/s, opposite in sign) and the stellar field was a quarter to an eighth as strong; coupling was 0.1–0.5% of today's. Since the ingestion that spun the star up, the current system has reversed handedness and risen about 200–800 times. The environment, its life and its people are still adapting to that change.

The mean molecular weight is 31.5, the density at 288 K is 2.13 kg/m³ (1.74 times Earth's), the scale height 7.7 km and the total mass 8.84×10¹⁸ kg. The oxygen partial pressure equals Earth's. CO₂ at 0.008 atm is equivalent to 0.8% at Earth sea level, the edge of human tolerance, and it pools in sheltered basins. The narcotic load of the inert gases equals a dive of about 14 m on Earth (Ar 2.3, Kr 7.1 and Xe 25.6 times N₂'s potency), mild, and xenon is a negligible fraction of its anesthetic threshold. The 1.6 atm total, the 25% argon and the Kr/Xe enrichment are adopted premises with no natural source for the noble-gas excess.

**Oxygen.** The atmosphere holds 3.7×10¹⁹ mol of O₂, which Earth-like sinks would remove in 3.7 Myr (0.4–0.7 Myr with volcanic sinks 5–10 times stronger). Oxygen left by hydrogen escape is 50–500 times too small to hold it, so the silicon biosphere is the oxygen source (see [[Life on Middle Path]]).

**Argon and potassium.** The argon is radiogenic (K-40 to Ar-40), which is why the bulk composition is potassium-rich. Argon accumulated with time: 46% of today's inventory existed at 1 Gyr and 88% at 3 Gyr. It is held out of the active atmosphere in two ways: radiogenic argon stays locked in potassium minerals of cold crust until heated, and a glacial clathrate pump traps argon as air hydrate in thick, cold nightside ice that is buried under sediment; because hydrates do not survive slab temperatures, the pump is a delay line of hundreds of Myr to Gyr that returns argon at arc volcanoes. Seafloor clathrate formation from dissolved gas does not occur (argon hydrate is stable from 0.4 bar only below about 180–190 K).

**Surviving the star.** Atmospheric loss would be catastrophic, and the planet survives through the star's history. The star was a slow rotator until the ingestion 10 Myr ago, so the planet saw X-ray and ultraviolet flux of about 8.8 times Earth's (0.041 W/m²) for 4.49 Gyr and 293 times Earth's (1.36 W/m²) only for the last 10 Myr; had the star been saturated for the full 4.5 Gyr, even the mildest ion-loss scaling would have removed the atmosphere. Thermal escape of N₂ is negligible below an exobase of about 5,000 K, and atomic oxygen is the weak link, so CO₂ cooling of the thermosphere keeps it cool. Hydrogen loss is limited by the cold trap to 1–11% of the water in 4.5 Gyr. The strong dipole, the dome's 23% cut of the planet's XUV cross-section, volcanic resupply, a heavy N₂ column and carbonate sinks hold the rest. The loss rates are order-of-magnitude scalings, not a simulation.

## Water, ice and the nightside

The planet holds 20% of Earth's water, 2.8×10²⁰ kg, a 523 m layer over the whole planet; oceans cover about half the surface at a mean depth of about 1 km, and much more of the land is arid than on Earth. Atmospheric vapor is only 0.017% of the inventory.

Ice thickness is limited by the heat rising from below: at a surface temperature of 235 K, conduction allows about 1.8 km of ice over 0.05 W/m² of heat flow, 0.9 km at 0.1 W/m², 0.44 km at 0.2 W/m² and 0.25 km at 0.36 W/m²; at 255–265 K only 50–800 m. The planet's high heat flow therefore permits thick ice only over old, potassium-poor cratonic lithosphere. Cold air also carries little moisture (cap snowfall 0.002–0.06 m/yr), so ice caps are small and mobile.

**Nightside plateau ring.** An elevated plateau about 3 km high (worth about −18 K) forms a belt about 120–150° from the substellar point, between the antistellar oasis and the terminator. Ice over its low-flux cratons, at 235 K or colder, covers 2–5% of the planet and holds 2.5–9% of the water, so no nightside cold trap can drain the dayside. It is the site of the argon pump and the source of the katabatic outflow described below.

**The antistellar oasis.** At the antistellar point an ice-free ocean oasis of 1,000–2,000 km radius stays at about 265–275 K, kept warm by descending air, ocean currents and heat of about 0.5–2 PW (comparable to Earth's peak ocean heat transport), surrounded by the cold plateau ring.

## Climate and temperature

The dome leaves a global mean absorbed flux of 209 W/m² (272 W/m² unshaded). With perfect redistribution and no greenhouse the planet would sit at 246 K, so the atmosphere supplies about +42 K (Earth's is about 33 K): a gray greenhouse optical depth near 1.2 (Earth's 0.83) from 0.008 atm of CO₂ and water vapor in 1.6 atm of air, with heat transport of 5–10 W/m²/K carrying 35–44% of the absorbed power to the nightside. The result is a mean of about 288 K, a bright ring warmer and a far nightside cooler than that, and no snowball state: raising the ice albedo changes nothing because the nightside receives no sunlight. The moist-greenhouse limit is not reached (the global mean absorbed flux is below the ≈280 W/m² runaway limit and heat is exported).

The Stillpoint's full shadow covers only 0.03% of the planet, much less than the scale over which a dense, fast atmosphere mixes air, so it is a place of shade, not a cold spot: it removes direct starlight, glare, ultraviolet and flares. It is livable but not comfortable: at its edge people need technology and heat suits, and inside the shade they can remove their helmets. Heavy CO₂ may settle in its calm air.

## Circulation and weather

The circulation is a global overturning in the weak-temperature-gradient regime, with no narrow banded jets. About 10⁹ W per metre of terminator length crosses to the nightside, driving overturning winds of 3–28 m/s (typically 7–17 m/s).

1. **Eyewall.** Air rises in a ring about 5–15° (570–1,700 km) from the Stillpoint, where the sunlight gradient is steepest; an encircling mountain ring at about 600–1,000 km pins it permanently. The Stillpoint lies on the rotation equator where the Coriolis parameter is zero, so the eyewall is a radial convective ring with a weak twist on its northern and southern arcs, not a spinning hurricane.
2. **Calm eye.** Air sinks over the dome's shadow, so the Stillpoint and the dry eye around it are calm; winds and storms break around the encircling mountains.
3. **Journey aloft.** Air leaves the ring aloft toward the nightside, a path of 15,700–18,200 km that takes 10–21 days.
4. **Antistellar descent.** It descends at the antistellar point at 1–2 mm/s, where compression heating of about 90 K balances radiative cooling, and a warm oasis forms below.
5. **Cold return breeze.** Air flowing outward along the nightside surface cools toward an inversion-limited temperature over a 15–40 day trip and arrives at the terminator belt cold (≈243–268 K); drainage down the plateau ring adds 4–17 m/s of katabatic wind. This always-cold breeze from the dark crosses the belt and flows toward the ring, closing the loop. Because the breeze and the star share one axis, the shaded side of a ridge is its windward side, and the sunlit side is the calm lee where CO₂ can pool.

## Light, sky and sound

![[images/Life_near_the_terminator.png]]

*Life near the terminator*

The star gives 13,700–21,800 lux at 10–20° elevation (14–22% of full sun) and 1,000–1,100 W/m² of beam at the unshaded belt, 82% of it infrared, ten times the chronic eye-safety limit. Its light is red-weighted: of the photons in 400–700 nm, 57% are red, 32% green and 12% blue, and 700–750 nm adds 37% more. The melanopic efficacy is 0.43–0.58 of daylight per lux, a constant strongly alerting light, so sleep needs dark shelter.

The daytime sky is dusky: a dark peach to rose overhead and deeper salmon, copper or mauve toward the horizon, with a slightly metallic sheen from scattering in heavy, hazy, noble-gas-rich air (a qualitative description, not a computed spectrum). The twilight of the terminator is a ruddy, copper and purple glow. Auroras glow green, violet and crimson over the nightside and on the horizon toward the star in storms.

Sound travels well in the dense air (1.74 times Earth's density, acoustic impedance 1.7 times higher) at 331 m/s, so voices are about half a semitone lower, and wind and water sound rich and heavy.

## Habitability

Humans live in the belt, bounded inward by heat and glare and outward by cold and darkness. The hot ring is lethal without suits (wet-bulb 36.8 °C at 315 K and 70% humidity, above the 35 °C survivable limit), the star is a chronic eye hazard, calm CO₂ pools in caves, pits and lee valleys, flares reach the belt as light in 38 s, energetic particles in 77–383 s and a coronal ejection in 1.6–8 h, and the cold breeze from the dark gives wind chills of −30 to −34 °C at the dark edge. Water is the scarcest resource: agriculture uses more than 99% of human water. See [[Life on Middle Path]] for the biospheres, the people and their customs.

## Physical basis

**Bulk planet.** A gravity of exactly 1.00 g and a large iron core fix the mass and radius together: for a rocky planet 1.00 g gives R = √M in Earth units, and mass trades against core fraction. The core fraction comes from the approximate rocky mass–radius fit R = (1.07 − 0.21 CMF) M^0.27, not an interior-structure model, so a full model may shift the radius and density by a few percent; the core holds about 92% of Earth's core mass. A large core does not by itself guarantee a dynamo: convective power, the rotation (about 17 times slower than Earth's) and mantle behavior matter.

**Climate budget.** The design is a band, not a point. A coldest nightside above 273 K needs a heat-transport coefficient of at least 9 W/m²/K at a greenhouse optical depth τ = 1.2 (at least 15 at τ = 1.0 and 3.5 at τ = 2.0). Across the 5–10 W/m²/K range the bright ring peaks at about 315 K (D = 5) to 304 K (D = 10) and the far-nightside antipode sits at about 263 K to 275 K; the lower end leaves a possible cold cap on the far nightside (about a quarter of the planet below freezing) and the upper end leaves nowhere frozen. The nightside radiates 35–44% of the absorbed power. Whether 0.008 atm of CO₂ with water vapor delivers τ ≈ 1.2 is a rough judgment that awaits a radiative-transfer calculation.

**Dome and shadow.** The dome covers the star in proportion to the angle from the Stillpoint: about 19% of the star is visible at 10°, 42% at 20°, 63% at 30°, 84% at 44° and 97% at 60°. The dome is above the horizon from every point within 88.1° of the substellar point, and the nightside never sees it. At its mass the dome is a controllable solar sail, with an acceleration from sunlight of 1.4–2.8×10⁻⁶ m/s²; a storm gust 100 times the steady wind load shifts its equilibrium by about 0.6 km over three hours. Over a c-day the anchor stays inside the full shadow (an offset of 0.04° against a 0.07° margin), the full shadow stays within a few kilometers of 242 km radius and the stellar flux varies by ±2%. The model is the circular restricted three-body L1 point with the eccentric variation scaled from it; the tether dynamics are not solved.

**Argon, potassium and heat.** The argon is radiogenic (K-40 to Ar-40, 10.7% branching, half-life 1.25 Gyr). With 35–50% of the produced argon reaching the atmosphere, the silicate holds about 0.9–1.3 wt% potassium (40–60 times Earth's), and K-40 supplies 135–195 TW today (about 7 times that at 1 Gyr and 12 times at formation), a surface flux of 0.25–0.36 W/m² from K-40 alone against Earth's total of 0.09. About 15–22% of the potassium (0.5 wt% of the core) sits in the core, which keeps radiogenic mantle heating from starving the dynamo. Production now adds only about 5% of the all-time argon per Gyr.

**Argon sinks.** Removing 5% of the argon inventory per Gyr needs only about 0.016 mm/yr of ice carrying hydrate air. Below 1–1.5 km the bubble gas sits at 90–135 bar (argon fugacity 22–34 bar), and argon hydrate is stable below about 0.37 km of ice at 225 K, 0.68 km at 235 K, 1.2 km at 245 K and 2 km at 255 K, so the pump works only where the ice is cold and thick. The whole ocean holds only 0.26% of the atmosphere's argon, and neutron capture converts a negligible fraction (an e-folding time of about 5×10²² years in crustal flux; converting 1% of the argon would fission about Earth's entire uranium), so only trace local K-41 from natural-reactor-like deposits is credible. The hydrate dissociation pressures use remembered values and need a literature check.

**Escape.** The star spun at about 50 days until the ingestion, which gives 0.041 W/m² of XUV (8.8 times Earth's, assuming Lx/Lbol ≈ 3×10⁻⁵) for 4.49 Gyr and 1.36 W/m² (293 times) for the last 10 Myr. Thermal escape of N₂ reaches 1% over 4.49 Gyr only above an exobase near 11,700 K and is negligible below about 5,000 K; atomic oxygen loses 10⁻⁴ at 5,000 K and 11% at 7,500 K, so the thermosphere must stay cool. Ion loss, scaled crudely from Earth's ~10²⁵ ions/s, removes roughly 8% (1× scaling) to 80% (10×) of the atmosphere over the quiet and saturated eras together. The magnetopause stands at 4.1, 5.2 and 6.1 planet radii for 30, 60 and 100 μT. The energy-limited hydrodynamic bound (1–100 Myr for the whole atmosphere at saturated flux, 33–3,300 Myr when quiet) applies only to light-gas-driven escape and is not a prediction.

**Circulation.** The overturning is in the weak-temperature-gradient regime: thermal-adjustment parameter c/Ωa ≈ 1.8, Rossby number 0.4–1.1, equatorial deformation radius about 6,100 km and Rhines scale 2,800–4,800 km. Absorbed flux in the eyewall rises from about 4 W/m² at the umbra's edge to 74 W/m² at 570 km, 202 at 1,140 km and 324 at 1,700 km. The Coriolis parameter is about 1.5×10⁻⁶ s⁻¹ at 10° latitude, roughly 70 times weaker than Earth's mid-latitude value, and mean updrafts are 0.01–0.09 m/s for 5–10 m/s low-level inflow. Air cools only about 6–22 K on its journey aloft; it descends at the antistellar point in 40–70 days over 8 km, and the oasis needs about 0.5 PW (1,000 km radius) to 2 PW (2,000 km). The return breeze relaxes toward its inversion-limited temperature within 5–16 days of a 15–40 day trip. These are scaling estimates with assumed boundary-layer depth, inversion strength, slope and drag; the pattern needs a three-dimensional simulation, including its east–west structure (a Coriolis-driven equatorial jet is possible).

**Core and field.** The core has a mean density of 10.35 g/cm³ and an outer-core volume of 1.62×10²⁰ m³. Its resistivity stays near 1.2–1.3 μΩ·m whatever the additives (impurities raise it by at most about 10%), and in the energy-flux scaling the field does not depend on conductivity; light elements contribute through compositional buoyancy (about 1.2 TW of dynamo power) and a larger core. Core heat production is about 209 TW today (30 TW from K-40, 86 TW from uranium, 93 TW from thorium), 256 TW at 3.5 Gyr age and 574 TW at 1 Gyr age, which gives dynamo power of about 26, 32 and 70 TW at a conversion efficiency of 0.12. Whole-planet radiogenic heat is about 330–400 TW. At this rotation a physical dynamo would be multipolar (local Rossby number 1–4, dipole fraction about 0.1, surface dipole only 3–11 μT). The tidal axis lock raises the dipole fraction to 0.6, and the chiral additive acts as a rotation-independent α of about 19 m/s per meV of chiral chemical potential (about 40,000 times Earth's core flow), reaching the ohmic limit: a core rms field of about 13 mT and a surface equatorial dipole of about 144 μT today, 160 μT at 1 Gyr ago and 237 μT at 3.5 Gyr ago. Without the additive the same power gives about 82 μT; 500 μT needs 312 TW of ohmic dissipation and 1 mT needs 1,250 TW. Chirality would relax within picoseconds at core temperatures, so the additive is a declared premise, not physics.

**Adopted premises and limits.** The adopted world is a coherent, declared design supported by physical bookkeeping where available. Its premises, stated without being derived, are the 1.6-atm, 25%-argon and Kr/Xe-enriched atmosphere; the potassium-rich composition and uranium, thorium and potassium in the core; the tidal axis lock and chiral additive that make the dynamo dipolar and strong; the quiet star until the ingestion 10 Myr ago; the dome's ultra-light material and active control; the silicon biosphere and its chemistry; and the divinely transplanted carbon life. The climate and circulation are energy-balance and scaling estimates (one dimension, gray greenhouse, assumed boundary layers), and the wind pattern, the east–west structure and the radiative balance need three-dimensional modeling. Escape, coupling power, ice conduction, hydrate pressures, light and photosynthesis, and the biological numbers use remembered coefficients and assumed ranges. Convective-power and ohmic-limit scalings are calibrated on Earth with a rough dipole-fraction step, and whether the core can shed its radiogenic heat through the core–mantle boundary, and whether potassium, uranium and thorium enter the core, are open.

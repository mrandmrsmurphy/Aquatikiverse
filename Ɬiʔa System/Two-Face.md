# Two-Face

![[images/Two_Face.png]]

*Artist's impression of Two-Face: bronze-toned illuminated terrain, a dark frozen hemisphere and a shifting luminous twilight girdle. The illustration is evocative rather than a thermal or magnetic simulation.*

**Two-Face**, or **Mirar's Veil b / AQ-MV-01 b**, is the innermost planet in the [[Ɬiʔa System]]. It is a dense **super-Mercury** orbiting the fictional red dwarf [[Mirar's Veil]], approximately 50 light-years away toward Ophiuchus. An oversized iron-rich core, metal-bearing plains and a thin, enduring **0.02-atm atmosphere** distinguish it from an airless Mercury analogue.

Its principal contrast is between a hot, star-facing landscape and a permanently dark, deeply frozen hemisphere. Strongly illuminated terrain typically reaches **400–450 K**; exceptional small heated sites reach approximately **700 K**. The nightside is referenced to **160–190 K**, which allows an atmosphere to survive while the hemisphere remains visually dark and cold. A temperate strip lies somewhat dayward of the geometric terminator.

An irregular girdle of auroral arcs, electrically lit geysers, ionized dust and gas, and intermittent star-connected plasma tubes marks the twilight frontier. It is a variable composite display, not a continuous magnetic pole or an incandescent band around a molten planet. The intrinsic field and surrounding stellar plasma supply different parts of the phenomenon; neither an exotic insulating layer nor a planet-sized capacitor is required.

## Physical characteristics and metallic terrain

Two-Face's large iron-rich core leaves a comparatively thin silicate mantle, but that mantle remains a substantial rocky layer. Broad ultramafic exposures include olivine- and pyroxene-bearing terrains, with high-pressure mineral phases confined to appropriate deep environments. Sulfide veins, iron alloys and graphite-bearing zones contribute to the dark, metal-rich appearance.

Metallic plains include concentrated deposits, ancient metal-bearing flows and exceptional impact exposures; they need not be an exposed core everywhere. Iron-rich meteorites and metallic ejecta are conspicuous, but ordinary fragmented ultramafic rock and stone breccias also occur. The dark Bond-albedo reference is a heterogeneous regolith/terrain target, not the optical property of a perfectly polished global iron sphere.

A silicate mantle overlies a differentiated iron–nickel alloy core, potentially containing sulfur and other light elements. It may retain a convecting liquid region. Residual cooling and compositional buoyancy, with possible tidal contributions, supply the intrinsic dynamo budget. A comparatively radionuclide-poor metal-rich interior can still contain localized hotter silicate zones, but no heat production is quantified merely by naming an alloy. An interior model must establish core state, magnetic-field strength and thermal longevity.

There is no superionic Ice XI or hydrogen-bearing post-perovskite insulating shell, no whole-planet Leyden jar, permanent global charge reservoir or unsupported shear-energy source. Surface conductivity varies between exposed metal/mineralized fractures and more resistive regolith; localized charging and current closure do not require the core to rotate freely forever.

## Atmosphere

Two-Face keeps a **real 0.02-atm atmosphere**, and its nightside is warm enough that the envelope does not collapse into an exosphere. Its reference molecular backbone is **N₂ 55%, CO₂ 44.9%, CO 0.1%**. The backbone is rich in CO₂. Atomic oxygen and sodium are not bulk constituents (neither is viable at 30% or 15% of the atmosphere), but they are important in upper-atmosphere and plume emission. The three backbone fractions total 100%; introducing variable minor gases means correspondingly adjusting those normalized fractions, not appending large uncounted percentages.

Photochemistry, energetic-particle impacts and outgassing supply variable Na, K, O, water-derived species, hydrocarbons and nitriles. Sputtering, particle/photon-stimulated desorption and impacts can release metal atoms without requiring all 400-K rock to evaporate. Trace gas temperatures and upper-atmosphere plasma energies differ from the temperature of the ground. Acetylene, HCN, ethane, benzene and other nitrile-bearing compounds remain candidate local feedstocks or protected deposits; each requires a supply, destruction and phase budget rather than a fixed global abundance.

The nominal CO₂ partial pressure is approximately **910 Pa**. A check using published saturation data over its valid interval finds solid-CO₂ saturation pressures above this value between 154.26 and 190 K. Thus the adopted **160–190-K** night target does not by itself force CO₂ to deposit across the whole dark hemisphere. This is a phase-consistency check, not a climate solution; colder transient sites, high-altitude condensation and source/sink balance require separate modeling. [CO₂ phase data](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C124389&Mask=4)

Atmospheric circulation transports some day-side energy into darkness. A 180-K black surface radiates approximately 60 W/m²; applied to an entire hemisphere this is about 5.4% of the total absorbed starlight. This demonstrates that the desired temperature is not excluded by the global energy supply, but it is **not** a calculated transport efficiency or top-of-atmosphere cooling rate. A nongray atmospheric/circulation model must determine whether the selected mixture and pressure actually maintain that map.

The air is not breathable. It has no adopted free-oxygen reservoir capable of sustaining extensive ordinary combustion. The dark side is cold and lacks visible thermal incandescence; its darkness is not justified by an absolute claim that cold temperatures make all fire or electrical emission impossible. Rare discharges can illuminate particular nightside sites, leaving the general hemisphere dark between events.

The thin atmosphere does not automatically protect organic molecules from stellar UV. Particle chemistry can build complex products while photolysis destroys others; replenishment, aerosol screening, burial and mineral protection are necessary parts of the inventory. No long-term atmospheric-retention or chemical steady-state calculation has yet been completed.

## Surface environment and volatile reservoirs

### Illuminated hemisphere

Most strongly illuminated dark terrain is referenced to **400–450 K**. The simple nonredistributing gray-surface substellar estimate is approximately **444 K** at the adopted albedo, consistent with that scale before atmospheric effects. Bronze, gold-brown and dark metallic tones arise chiefly from reflected stellar light and surface composition, rather than uniform visible incandescence.

Approximately **700 K** is reached at small, exceptional geothermal, electrical-discharge or impact-heated sites. A black surface at 700 K needs about **11,400 W/m²** beyond the local absorbed starlight. That power is not supplied over continents. A 0.01–1-km² footprint would require roughly 10⁸–10¹⁰ W at that reference excess, before transient thermal storage and geometry. These local requirements fit inside the selected event bookkeeping allowance but are not derived current-deposition or geothermal solutions. The discharge plasma can be far hotter than the surrounding rock without heating a large area of ground to its plasma temperature.

### Twilight frontier

A local **270–300-K** strip lies modestly on the dayward side of the illumination boundary. In the airless reference the corresponding locations are approximately **8–12° dayward**; the atmosphere, relief, albedo and geothermal anomalies shift them. The geometric terminator itself is not assigned an Earth-like temperature simply because it is a boundary. Finite stellar angular size and optical libration broaden and move the illumination transition.

Vents and fractures intersect this frontier, releasing volatile gases and organic-bearing dust. Sublimation/deposition fronts, aerosol plumes and electrical currents overlap there. Organic fogs, ice-grain showers and occasional local condensate events are possible where the species' actual temperature and partial pressure permit; there is no assumed planet-wide liquid methane or benzene rain at arbitrary conditions.

### Dark hemisphere

The typical night target is now **160–190 K**. Dark tholin-like deposits, polycyclic aromatic material, heavier frozen organics, nitrile-rich sediments and mixed mineral/water-ice terrain preserve its blackened visual character. Water ice may be buried under dark lag or exposed in local scarps; a frozen hemisphere need not have a uniformly bright reflective surface.

**There are no exposed nitrogen ice seas or exposed methane/ethane/propane ice layers on the ordinary surface.** At 160–190 K those phases cannot remain exposed. Nitrogen belongs chiefly to the atmosphere or to possible buried mineral/clathrate inventories; lighter hydrocarbons occur as gases, buried trapped species, or transient products rather than kilometer-thick exposed frost layers. Their detailed phase and loss histories remain unresolved.

The deep reservoirs are **layered, buried volatile-rich basins**, including protected organic strata, water-rich ice, potential clathrates and localized ammonia-bearing cryobrines under suitable pressure and geothermal conditions. No uniform thickness or inventory is assigned to nitrogen clathrates without a phase calculation. Reservoirs can feed fissures and cryovolcanic venting, with freezing, degassing or evaporation as the fluids reach low-pressure terrain. Basal and surface conditions need not be the same. These deposits are the source of episodic plumes and sediment recycling, not an unmodeled global frozen nitrogen ocean.

## Magnetic field and plasma environment

The nominal **60-μT equatorial intrinsic field** can have a strongly tilted axis and multipolar structure. A nominal dipole tilt of **85°** places its poles near the geographical equator, but it does not place a continuous magnetic maximum along the full day–night boundary. Pole longitude relative to the star, stellar-field orientation and field variability all matter. “Equatorial,” “magnetic” and “terminator” are separate geometrical labels.

A simple pressure-balance estimate using the adopted stellar wind gives a magnetic obstacle of approximately **three planetary radii**. This is an illustrative stand-off scale, not an MHD solution or a guarantee of shielding. Interaction can open or reconnect field lines, create Alfvénic current systems and accelerate particles. Magnetic induction, core dynamo generation and atmospheric current closure have different sources and efficiencies; high conductivity does not automatically increase every form of heat deposition.

### The luminous girdle

The twilight display combines several processes:

- **Auroral curtains and bright knots:** particle-driven emission at current footprints and favorable magnetic latitudes.
- **Diffuse transition-zone glow:** upper-atmosphere/exospheric emission, recombination and illuminated aerosols near the day–night transition.
- **Ionized gas and dusty plasma:** volatile vents inject material into a structured electromagnetic environment.
- **Localized discharges:** charge separation in dusty plumes, heterogeneous surface materials and current systems permits brief ground-connected flashes without a planet-sized capacitor.

The result is a broken, drifting, sometimes nearly encircling luminous girdle, with bright sectors and darker gaps. It is not a permanently uniform fire-ring or an actual particulate ring. Blue, green, violet and other spectral features depend on the excited species and particle energies; sodium can contribute strong yellow-orange light, while dark brown/black particulate columns are seen by reflected light or silhouette. Exact colors, opacity and naked-eye brightness await emission/photochemical modeling.

## Intermittent flux tubes and lightning geysers

Star-connected plasma tubes form, brighten, twist and reconnect in favorable sectors. During active intervals they produce energetic-particle precipitation, elevated radio emission, luminous upper-atmosphere jets and intense local discharges. Coronal eruptions can trigger events, but ordinary changes in wind and magnetic topology can also do so; not every stellar flare ejects a CME that strikes the planet.

Venting fractures can become **lightning geysers**: expanding gas, dust and icy material are briefly lit by discharges and particle excitation. “Plasma fires” names their appearance, not unconfined hydrocarbon combustion in oxygen-rich air. Transient chemistry can produce excited molecular emission, chemiluminescent fogs, HCN-bearing products/polymers and organic fallout where reaction pathways and local shielding permit. Ionized material can respond to fields while neutral gas and grains also follow pressure, gravity and drag; magnetic field lines are not rails that automatically guide all convection.

The selected bookkeeping model assigns a **high-connection total circuit power of 10¹⁴ W**, a **1% high-state duty fraction**, and a **10¹¹-W quiescent circuit reference**. These are design inputs, not observed rates or derived efficiencies. High-state allocations of up to **10¹³ W** deposited in the planet's environment and **10¹¹ W** of visible emission are allowances inside that total budget, not predictions that all events achieve them. The total includes power dissipated at stellar and planetary footprints and in the intervening plasma; it is not all deposited at the ground.

The available intercepted dimensional Poynting-flux scale is approximately **1.7×10¹⁵ W** in the same toy wind. Staying below that scale avoids assigning more power than this illustration supplies, but does not establish coupling, current closure or emission efficiencies. The field need not stay magnetically linked in a high-current state between visible events. The stellar spin/orbital beat reference is approximately **2.42 days**, organizing possible activity windows without making storms exactly periodic.

### Orbital angular-momentum budget

The violent flux tubes do not drive rapid orbital decay. The current star rotates every **two days**, while Two-Face orbits every **11.6 days**. Its orbit is outside the stellar corotation radius, approximately **0.0182 AU**. A direct link to the faster-rotating star therefore tends to transfer stellar angular momentum **outward to the orbit**, not necessarily brake the planet inward. Other wind/plasma torque components may have different signs. [Magnetic star–planet torque models](https://doi.org/10.1088/0004-637X/795/1/86)

For the simple circular direct-link budget, total dissipation and torque are related by **P = (Ωstar − n)τ**, and the local semimajor-axis change timescale is **Lorb/(2|τ|)**. With the selected duty cycle and quiescent allowance, mean circuit dissipation is approximately **1.10×10¹² W**, implying a local migration timescale of approximately **2.6×10¹⁵ years**. Even assigning the selected 10¹⁴-W high state continuously gives roughly **2.9×10¹³ years** under this same model. These long values are conditional estimates, not an integrated history, but they show that the adopted spectacle does not require rapid magnetic orbital evolution.

Dividing the orbital binding energy by the total circuit luminosity would be wrong, because the dissipated power is shared between stellar-spin work and orbital work. These conditional timescales mean the selected budget needs no rapid orbital decay or differential migration, and intermittency lowers the time-averaged torque further.

A complete model must include vector wind torques, time-varying fields, stellar braking, planetary tides and interaction with the c-centered chain. The brief modern strong-field state need not have existed throughout 4.5 Gyr. Slow differential migration can still affect resonance, and the budget is not proof that the entire chain remains stable indefinitely.

## Observability

From the adopted c reference orbit, circular coplanar separation extrema give a geometrical diameter of approximately **17.7 to 2.4 arcminutes**. At closest separation that is slightly more than half Earth's Moon's apparent diameter. At large distance an unaided observer may perceive only a bright point despite the finite disk; phase and reflected-light contrast affect brightness independently of angular size.

The illuminated hemisphere appears bronze-toned and patchily reflective. The dark hemisphere has no general visible thermal glow, while localized discharges and faint aurorae can punctuate its darkness. From nearby space the transition-zone curtains, vent flashes and drifting plasma structures provide the brightest dynamic accents. The conspicuous girdle is a source of visual identity, not a quantified naked-eye detection from every viewing geometry.

Roche values depend on the assumed material, so there is no unique radius. The tidal output is illustrative, not a dissipation model or proof that the eccentricity is maintained; k₂ and Q are uncertain and depend on frequency and structure. These numbers do not establish the core's longevity, the field's tilt or its intensity.

**Phase check.** The CO₂ saturation pressure uses the NIST Antoine fit (A = 6.80657, B = 1,301.679, C = −3.494, with pressure in atm and temperature in K), valid only between 154.26 and 195.89 K and not extrapolated below. At the lower limit it is about 1,508 Pa, already above the 910-Pa CO₂ partial pressure, so the 160–190 K target is phase-consistent; this does not show resistance to atmospheric collapse under every circulation or cloud state. The 180-K radiation figure is a surface diagnostic, not top-of-atmosphere radiation or transport power, and the atmosphere absorbs and reemits infrared while the night is not uniform, so a nongray radiative, photochemical and circulation model is still needed. A protected volatile deposit can still lose material through cracks and needs a quantified supply and escape history. The 400–450 K ordinary surface and the 700-K sites are design targets: a high event-power allocation gives a finite possible budget but does not calculate current focusing, rock thermal inertia or vent emissivity.

**Limits.** The arithmetic, phase comparison, normalized composition, power allocations and torque identities are checked. They do not validate an atmosphere over 4.5 Gyr, a 75%-core equation-of-state fit, a 60-μT dynamo, complete stellar wind and current closure, visible brightness, emission colors, climate or long-term planetary resonance.

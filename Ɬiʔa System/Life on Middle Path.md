# Life on Middle Path

![[images/Glider_and_Butterflies.png|300]]

*A glider and butterflies: a representative illustration of Middle Path life.*

**Life on Middle Path** is the pair of independent, coexisting biospheres of the planet [[Middle Path (exoplanet)|Middle Path]], orbiting the red dwarf [[Mirar's Veil]]. One is ancient and silicon-based, native to the planet, arisen by abiogenesis about 3 billion years ago and living in the dry, bright, electrically restless regions around the substellar point. The other is carbon-based and Earth-derived: humans and a curated suite of plants and animals, transplanted by divine act less than 10,000 years ago into the temperate belt around the terminator. They differ in chemistry, history and range, yet share one atmosphere and one electromagnetic environment; the silicon biosphere in fact makes the air breathable for the carbon one. Numbers are mostly scaling arithmetic; see the model limits at the end.

## Overview

Middle Path is tidally locked, with a dense noble-gas-rich atmosphere and a very light hemispherical dome, the Tethered Moon, shading the substellar point. Efficient heat transport keeps the planet temperate on average (288 K) with a hot ring around a shaded center and a cool nightside. The dry, calm **eye** around the Stillpoint, the humid **eyewall** pinned by encircling mountains, and the hot, bright **ring** (304–315 K, up to 820 W/m² of beam) are the silicon biosphere's world; the belt from the terminator to about 55° from the Stillpoint is the carbon biosphere's. Roughly half the surface is shallow ocean holding 20% of Earth's water, and the rest is rock, basaltic plains and crystalline highlands.

## Origin of life

**Silicon.** The native biosphere arose about 3 billion years ago through abiogenesis in the dry, hot, infrared-bright, electrically active conditions near the substellar region. Self-organizing siloxane and silicate structures that could harvest radiation and charge gradients were favored, and evolution rewarded organisms that tolerated thermal cycling, electrical surges and oxidation of reduced silicon compounds. The potassium-rich alkaline chemistry of the planet supplies natural ferroelectric minerals (KNbO₃ with a Curie temperature of 691 K, BaTiO₃ with 393 K).

**Carbon.** The carbon biosphere is **transplanted**: less than 10,000 years ago humans, antediluvian descendants of Enoch in a long-lived, slow-aging form, and a curated suite of Earth plants, animals and microbes were set by divine act in the stable climate of the terminator belt. Divine transplantation and abiogenesis are separate premises; neither is converted into panspermia. The carbon biota share standard terrestrial biochemistry, adapted to the star's light and the dense, CO₂-richer air. The two biospheres are entirely separate in origin and chemistry, yet exchange energy and information indirectly through the shared atmosphere and electromagnetic environment.

## Silicon biosphere

### Energy, chemistry and the oxygen source

**Light.** Silicon life's primary energy is near-infrared photovoltaic photosynthesis. The star's 850–1000 nm band carries 10.8% of its flux (700–1100 nm 28%; beyond 1100 nm 61%). A silicon-gap (1.12 eV) converter reaches a detailed-balance limit of about 19% and gaps of 0.7–0.9 eV about 24%. In-band photon flux is about 210 μmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 10° from the Stillpoint, 450 at 20° and 690 at the ring peak (Earth's full PAR is about 2000).

**Electrosynthesis is a supplement and a signaling medium.** The fair-weather air–earth current is about a picoampere per square metre, auroral precipitation stops more than 100 km up, and oxidizing rock yields only 10⁻⁴–10⁻³ W/m². Lightning, triboelectric charging and aurora light add small amounts of energy but carry rich information.

**Chemistry.** Silicon life is dry-phase, using mineral surfaces, porous silicate matrices and thin films of ionic or polymeric gel to mediate reactions, with percolating conduction networks and phase transitions in place of bulk liquids. Boundaries are **siloxane lattices** and doped silicate layers that act at once as skeleton, semipermeable membrane and charge-regulating interface, with crystalline defects, dopant bands and layered clays forming compartments. Free silane (SiH₄, 47 MJ/kg) is pyrophoric and cannot persist in 0.21 atm of oxygen, so it exists only at gas vents, where vent-creepers oxidize it (SiH₄/SiO₂ is their dominant redox couple). The stored biomass, "silicon kerogen", is **armored hydrogenated amorphous silicon and polysilane**: energy-rich (32 MJ/kg on oxidation to SiO₂) but protected by a passivating oxide skin. Ferrosilicate and charge-gradient cycling gives slow storage between storms and auroral events, and silicon organisms tolerate wide temperature ranges and can enter quasi-glassy or crystalline sleep during unfavorable field states.

**The oxygen source.** The atmosphere holds 3.7×10¹⁹ mol of O₂, which Earth-like sinks would remove in 3.7 Myr (0.4–0.7 Myr with volcanic sinks). Oxygen left by hydrogen escape is 50–500 times too small to hold it, so the silicon biosphere sustains the O₂: reducing silicon from silica and water costs about 7.9 eV per O₂ (about 12 near-infrared photons at 50% efficiency), and a net source of 10¹³–10¹⁴ mol/yr needs a production of 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ mol O₂/yr at 1% burial, 0.02–0.2% of absorbed starlight (Earth's biosphere uses about 0.07%). The burial of silicon kerogen is the net oxygen source, and humans found breathable air because of it.

### Habitat

The core habitat is the **dry eye** between the Stillpoint and the encircling mountains, where subsiding air is desert-dry (surface humidity about 0.01–4%) and partial light falls on the ground. Crystalwalker and vent-creeper communities extend into the humid, stormy eyewall and the hot, bright ring wherever gas vents and dry crystalline uplands occur; ground temperatures in the Hot Zone run to about 330 K. Community structure follows field topology as much as geometry: habitats are regions of stable bias in electric and magnetic fields.

### Lifeforms

- **Crystalwalkers.** Ground-dwelling crystalline organisms whose bodies are branching, articulated silicate structures. They move slowly by rearranging lattice defects, flexing piezoelectric spines and liquefying micro-layers at contact points. Some keep persistent "memory" in their doping patterns and can re-route their conduction pathways.
- **Vent-creepers.** Silicon-rooted, tendril-like forms clustered around gas vents and fissures. They absorb warm gases, charged dust and infrared radiation, oxidize vent silane, extend their tendrils toward field maxima and bright regions, and form dense forests of conductive filaments that sway with electromagnetic and mechanical tides.
- **Electric Choirs.** Aerial ferroelectric and piezoelectric organisms that soar in the eyewall updrafts and ring winds. A 1 m/s updraft gives a glider about 10 W/kg (Earth's birds use 5–20 W/kg), wind carries 0.6–2.1 kW/m² (Betz-limited) at 10–15 m/s, and their membranes harvest it piezoelectrically; ferroelectric photovoltaic wings add starlight. Their bodies are tuned antennas and resonators. Many Choir individuals are neurally simple alone but belong to **distributed hive intelligences**, where cognition emerges as patterns of harmonic resonance across a swarm.

### The Choirs' voice and the recent change

The Choirs signal on the planet's own resonances: Schumann-like modes at 7.6, 13.2 and 18.7 Hz, the electron cyclotron frequency (4.0 MHz at the equator and 8.1 MHz at the pole for the present field) and body resonances from about 25 kHz (10 cm) to 250 Hz (10 m), which lie in the audible band. Dayside ionospheric critical frequencies of about 20–37 MHz (scaled to 293 times Earth's XUV) reflect the 4–8 MHz band, so it travels around the planet.

The Choirs **awakened** with the ingestion of a giant planet by the star 10 Myr ago. Until then the planet outran a slower, weaker stellar field and the star–planet coupling was 0.1–0.5% of today's; since then it has risen about 200–800 times and reversed sense, with polarity flips every 2.26 days. That is 10⁴–10⁷ generations for lifetimes of 1,000 to 1 year, so the silicon biosphere is still adapting to the new electromagnetic regime.

## Carbon biosphere

### Plants

Plants, adapted to the star in color and chlorophyll, receive continuous light from one fixed direction. Of the photons in 400–700 nm, 57% are red, 32% green and 12% blue, and 700–750 nm adds 37% more; the red to far-red ratio is 0.78 (Earth's sunlight 1.15), a permanent shade-avoidance cue. Chlorophyll d/f-type pigments extend absorption through 700–750 nm, adding about 37% more usable photons and making leaves very dark; blue is scarce, so phototropic, stomatal and cryptochrome responses are tuned to the little there is. The photosynthetically active photon flux at 1.6 atm is:

**Morphology varies with angle to the star.** Near the terminator the standard plant form is **vertical and star-facing**: blades and canopies tilted toward the star (tilt from horizontal about 90° minus the star's elevation: 80° at 10° elevation, 70° at 20°, 60° at 30°) with a reflective or low-absorbing back face that scatters light into the canopy. Inward from about 60° the tilt relaxes toward horizontal, and inside about 45° leaves turn edge-on to the star and become reflective to shed heat and excess light. Horizontal leaves work only at about 65° and beyond the terminator-side limit.

**Gas.** The 0.008 atm of CO₂ (20 times Earth's partial pressure) raises light-limited net carbon gain per electron by about 47–50% and cuts photorespiration from 30% to 1.5–3% of carboxylation; stomata can run half closed, which saves water in the dry cold wind. High-CO₂ crops are expected to be carbon-rich and low in protein and minerals.

**Time and zones.** The ±1.15° libration of the star each c-day changes horizontal light by about ±11%, a mild c-day season that crops follow. The **agricultural zone** lies about 55–75° from the Stillpoint (star elevation 15–35°, ground at about 292–307 K, daily light 21–62 mol m⁻² d⁻¹ with the adapted pigments); its terminator edge is limited by low light and the cold breeze, and the hot ring inside 45° is too bright and hot for most crops.

### Animals and ecology

**The voluntary winter.** Many animals take a long winter by walking across the terminator to hibernate in the dark, where they are less likely to be disturbed. The hibernation grounds at 95–120° from the Stillpoint are dark and 272–286 K, which suits torpor. Routes and distances vary as much as on Earth, from a few hundred kilometers across the dayfall strip to 2,280–6,840 km walks from the belt; humans see most often the ones from the agricultural zone. At assumed speeds a 100 kg animal needs 91–273 days one way (5–16 c-days) and a 1,000 kg animal 76–228 days; a 10 kg animal 230–680 days; a 1 kg animal cannot make the longest walks. The calendar gives the rhythm: only the great year (939.4 days, about 2.57 years) holds a long round trip plus torpor, so the long winter of large animals (about 100 kg and up) is locked to the great year, while small animals hibernate locally or in nearer niches. Walking over 5,000 km costs a 100 kg animal fat equal to 17% of its mass without feeding (1,000 kg: 8%); the breeze blows toward the ring and the star, so outbound walkers face a headwind (+15% cost at 5 m/s, +52% at 10 m/s) and the return is downwind. A great-year torpor at 5% of basal metabolism needs about 12% of body mass in fat for 100 kg.

**Dens.** Choosing or building the den is crucial for people and animals alike: sealed dens are lethal even in deep torpor (a 100 kg animal in a sealed 20 m³ den adds 2.6% CO₂ by mole, 0.042 atm, in 30 days and 8.7% in 100 days). Keeping the added partial pressure to 0.01 atm needs about 0.83 m³ of sealed volume per kilogram for 30 days (2.8 m³/kg for 100 days) or a ventilated cave with a high intake and a floor-level drain. Snow or ice blocking a vent is a natural killer.

**Dayfall strip.** At the east and west terminator longitudes a strip about 261 km wide sees the star set for 4.5–16 days of each 17.4-day c-day (8.7 days at its center), a natural night for small animals.

**Productivity and biomes.** Light-limited potential production is 520–3,600 g C/m²/yr, but water limits it: mean evaporation is about 0.66 m/yr (Earth about 1.0), giving roughly 260–1,060 g C/m²/yr. Forests grow near coasts and rivers, savannah and steppe elsewhere, and much more land than on Earth is arid and barren, though never empty, as Earth's deserts are not. Trophic transfer is about 10% per step. Traveling Stillpoint-ward crosses very different biomes: the temperate terminator edge, the agricultural savannah and steppe (55–75°), the hot, humid ring where carbon life thins and silicon communities increase, the wet eyewall, and the dry eye of the silicon biosphere. Past the terminator lie the cold dark nightside with its hibernation grounds, the plateau ring and the antistellar oasis; its primary energy is not photosynthesis, so its ecosystems depend on imported biomass, geothermal or chemical energy and migrating animals.

### Humans

**Physiology and air.** The air is 1.74 times denser than Earth's: peak ventilation is about 0.76 of Earth's, voices sit about half a semitone lower and convective heat loss is about 12% greater. Physiology follows partial pressure, so at 1.6 atm each toxic CO₂ level needs only 0.625 times the added gas: the 0.008 atm baseline is 0.5% by mole, 0.04 atm (immediately dangerous) is 2.5%, 0.07 atm (confusion) 4.4% and 0.10 atm (unconsciousness) 6.3%. Heavy CO₂ pools weakly (5% added CO₂ makes air only 2% denser), and winds above 0.4–4.4 m/s clear pools 5–50 m deep, so only sheltered pits, caves and deep lee valleys are lethal on calm nights: a stark valley can be deadly.

**Dark shelter.** The star's light is a constant, strongly alerting stimulus (13,700–21,800 lux, melanopic efficacy 0.43–0.58 of daylight), so dark shelter is necessary for sleep and must be built correctly. Caves are natural, but eight hours in a sealed 30 m³ space with two people reaches 0.018 atm of CO₂ (headaches), a sealed 10 m³ space with two people 0.038 atm (near dangerous) and four people 0.069 atm (very dangerous). An air change of 0.5 per hour holds these to 0.015–0.023 atm, so good shelters have a high intake, a floor-level drain and raised sleeping places. There are no days, only the planetary calendar: sleep, work and meals follow culture, not a daily cycle.

**Eyes.** Infrared makes up 82% of the star's beam, about 1,000 W/m² without water absorption, 10 times the chronic corneal and lens limit, so looking toward the star is a chronic cataract and retinal hazard. Water, ice, polished rock and dust reflect the beam toward the eye (calm water reflects 58%, 35%, 13% and 6% at 5°, 10°, 20° and 30° star elevation), so **reflective surfaces silently blind people** and are very taboo and carefully managed. Protection strategies are:

- wide-brimmed hats with many dangling frills that break the line of sight to the low star;
- slit goggles in the Inuit style (a 1 mm slit against a 6 mm pupil admits about one sixth of the light);
- iron-rich green glass (the iron absorbs near-infrared), which cuts the infrared only 1.1–3.1 times, partial protection for everyday glare;
- smoky quartz and obsidian slivers;
- muscovite or biotite mica sheets washed with soot or mineral pigment, which at 0.5 g/m² or more reach optical depth 4 in the visible and 1.5–2.5 in the infrared and are the star-viewing filters, though they leave everything else nearly black.

Direct viewing needs at least 8.2 times attenuation of the infrared. A star-viewing screen projected through a telescope aperture up to about 10–20 cm is safe; 30 cm scorches the screen.

**Heat, cold and fixed shade.** A standing person in the beam absorbs 210–230 W of radiant heat in dark clothing and 105–115 W in reflective clothing, so clothing is light and shade is sought. The hot ring is lethal without suits (wet-bulb 36.8 °C at 315 K and 70% humidity). The cold breeze from the dark gives wind chills of −30 to −34 °C at the dark edge. Ground at low sun warms only a few kelvin, while star-facing walls take 870–1,000 W/m² and warm 20–22 K if dark and 7–8 K if white, so star-facing walls are white. The star never moves, so shade is permanent: a wall casts a shadow 11.4, 5.7, 2.7 and 1.7 times its height at 5°, 10°, 20° and 30° elevation, and permanent, fixed, safe shaded places are attractive to build. Because breeze and star share an axis, the shaded side of a ridge is its windward side and the sunlit side the calm lee where CO₂ can pool; valleys running toward the star are glare tunnels, and valleys running across it are shaded and cross-ventilated. Calm water looked at toward the star is hazardous in the outer belt, so people stand with their backs to the star at water and cover canals and cisterns.

**Water, food and population.** Food needs 380–2,600 m² of cropland and 190–1,800 m³ of water per person per year while drinking needs only 1.5 m³, so agriculture uses more than 99% of human water and water governance and storage dominate society. The ceiling set by food and water is 0.16–5.3 billion people, but humans are extraordinarily long-lived and the world is very stable, so children are rare and the global population in the primitive-technology period is under one million.

**Warnings.** A flare reaches the belt as light in 38 s, energetic particles in 77–383 s and a coronal mass ejection in 1.6–8 h.

### Technology and sky

Electricity and magnetism come first: the aurora, lightning, ground currents and a 144 μT field are obvious and reliable, and the technology tree runs nearly opposite to Earth's.

- **Compass.** The needle points at the Stillpoint everywhere on the dayside (field 144 μT at the terminator, 203 μT at 55°, dip 0–54°); a tangent galvanometer needs 8–11 mA with 1,000 turns, 5–7 times less sensitive than on Earth.
- **Telluric telegraph.** Quiet-day ground fields of at least 1 V/km give 50–500 V over 50–500 km of line and about 40 mA through 4 mm² iron wire (0.2 A through copper), enough for a telegraph sounder with no battery; storm fields of 50 V/km or more give kilowatts and destroy equipment.
- **Radio.** The Choirs are first heard by radio, singing in from the direction no one can bear to go, like angels from the sky.
- **Energy.** The surface breeze has a fixed direction toward the ring, so windmills need no yaw gear (79 W/m² at 5 m/s, 1.74 times Earth's; 25 kW for a 20 m rotor). A fixed star lets static concentrating mirrors reach 1,150 K at a concentration of 100 and 2,050 K at 1,000 (iron melts at 1,811 K), so smelting needs no fuel; because flat mirrors send eye-damaging beams for 45 m (1 m mirror) to 4.5 km (100 m), mirrors are confined to fenced, carefully managed mirror yards.
- **Navigation and sky.** The star's elevation gives the distance from the Stillpoint and the compass its bearing; the stars turn 0.86° per hour as a slow clock, and the neighboring planets keep the calendar. The Tethered Moon hangs directly below the star, and the Shield Couple is a naked-eye twin (see [[Middle Path (exoplanet)]]).
- **Optics.** A 3 cm spyglass resolves 3.9″ (215 km on the star) and shows star spots, a 10 cm telescope 1.2″ (1,200 km on the Shield Couple), a 30 cm telescope 0.39″. One arcsecond is 56 km on the star.

## Interfaces between the biospheres

Direct chemical interaction is limited: silicon organisms stay in the dry eye, the eyewall and the ring, and carbon organisms are limited by heat on the inner dayside and by cold and darkness on the nightside. They share the same atmosphere (carbon life breathes the silicon biosphere's oxygen and is touched by auroral chemistry and NOₓ residues), the same magnetospheric environment (storms and aurora shape both silicon electrosynthetic cycles and carbon biological rhythms), and occasional physical overlap where ring highlands meet the belt.

The Hot Zone is mostly hidden from the belt by the planet's curvature: a 3 km mountain sees 221 km, a 30 km balloon 700 km, and seeing a ground point 3,000 km away needs 605 km of height. People travel inward, and from a high mountain at the habitable edge they can spot strange activity at the ring's outer margin, but it is not a major part of early lore. The aurora is the exception: the storm oval is seen from inside 47–57° and the flare oval from inside 57–67° of the Stillpoint, so only storms and flares light the horizon toward the star. Physical contact is hard because the way inward is heat (the ring is lethal without suits), glare, storms and walking toward the star, which stays ahead and climbs higher with every step, with the breeze at the walker's back.

## Intelligent species

**Humans.** Earth-derived, long-lived and slow-aging, adapted to the dense, CO₂-richer air, with a culture deeply integrated with the planet's electromagnetic and auroral phenomena; their technology emphasizes electricity, magnetism and field manipulation earlier and more strongly than Earth's history did.

**Electric Choirs.** Distributed silicon hive intelligences: each Choir is many glider-like organisms whose bodies resonate at specific frequencies, and cognition emerges from interference patterns, harmonic locking and slow reconfiguration of resonance networks across the swarm. The Choirs think in waveforms, rhythms and harmonic structures rather than words, and communicate through modulated electromagnetic pulses, changes in resonance and controlled flashes of auroral excitation.

**Contact.** Early encounters were with crystalwalkers and vent-creepers at the Hot Zone's fringes, seen as "living stones" and "moving glass forests". The Choirs eventually responded to human radio emissions and deliberate beacons, establishing simple and then more elaborate resonance codes. Translation remains partial and brittle: humans can encode simple structural concepts, motions and rhythms into modulated fields, and the Choirs answer with repeatable waveform motifs interpretable as attention, agreement, warning or shared experience. Both species stay in their ecological bands, direct interference is rare, and the details of contact are reserved for later development.

## The Stillpoint and the tether

At the heart of the substellar region lies the **Stillpoint**: a dolomitic and crystalline basin permanently shaded by the Tethered Moon, about 242 km in radius, where the star is totally eclipsed with its corona as an aureole. Its air is calmer than the surrounding eyewall; winds and storms break around the encircling mountains. At its edge people need technology and heat suits, and inside the shade they can take off their helmets. Its ecology is sparse, slow-growing silicon crusts and deep glassy deposits, with occasional Choir overflights using the basin's field geometry as a reference and almost no carbon life under natural conditions.

Rising from the Stillpoint is the active **tether**, about 192,000 km long, tapering toward the dome, which stabilizes the moon and acts as a conduit and as the cultural axis mundi for humans and Choirs alike: the dipole axis, the compass, the star, the tether and the auroral ovals all center on this point. Many human traditions speak of emissaries ascending the tether to a great room where it meets the dome and of an ancient message or presence there; the content varies by account and is a focus of theology, philosophy and fiction, and remains tradition rather than confirmed fact. The Stillpoint and tether are widely treated as sacred or taboo.

## Model limits

The silicon chemistry (armored hydrogenated silicon, ferroelectric membranes, photovoltaic photosynthesis) and the Choirs' resonance cognition are speculative biology, not demonstrated self-replicating mineral chemistry; photosynthetic efficiencies, burial fraction, oxygen sinks, Choir transmitter powers and the biological and physiological numbers are assumed ranges or textbook values quoted from memory. Divine transplantation, the long-lived physiology of the humans, the dome's origin and the traditions of the message are declared premises and traditions. Plant adaptation, animal migration and den numbers are allometric scalings; no population, hydrology, ecosystem or circadian model is run.

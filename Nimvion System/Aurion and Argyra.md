# Aurion and Argyra

**Aurion and Argyra** are a pair of co-orbital gas giants sharing the same nearly circular orbit at 4.1 AU around the star [[Nimvion 3421]]. They occupy a proposed Trojan configuration with *Aurion* leading *Argyra* by about 60°. Relative to the star–Argyra pair, Aurion librates around the leading triangular equilibrium. Relative to the star–Aurion pair, Argyra occupies the trailing equilibrium. The two planets differ in mass, radius, and cloud chemistry, producing contrasting visual appearances: Aurion is a golden, moderately reflective giant with a faint dark ring, whereas Argyra is a silvery, high-albedo ringed world with prominent icy rings.

## Co-orbital Configuration
Together Aurion and Argyra have a combined mass of 350 M🜨 (≈1.1 M♃). Around a 0.93 M☉ star, this total is well within the classical Gascheau stability limit for Trojan configurations, supporting the plausibility of the triangular configuration. Finite libration amplitude and perturbations from the other planets require separate assessment.

The nominal orbital parameters of the co-orbital pair are:

| Parameter | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Semimajor axis (both) | 4.1 AU |
| Orbital period | ≈8.6 Earth years |
| Libration amplitude (angular separation) | ≈60° ± 30° |
| Eccentricity | ≈0.02 |
| Inclination | ≈2° (nearly coplanar with the system) |

The angular separation between the planets oscillates around 60°, with a libration amplitude of about ±30°. This places their mutual separation between roughly *2.1 AU* (at ≈30°) and *5.8 AU* (at ≈90°). They never approach closely enough for strong mutual tidal distortion, but their gravity fields interact over long timescales, influencing dust, Trojan swarms, and the outer small-body population of the [[Nimvion System]].

At 4.1 AU from a 0.80 L☉ star, the incident stellar flux is about 4.8% of Earth’s. For Bond albedos in the range 0.28–0.35, the resulting radiative equilibrium temperatures are about *120 K for Aurion* and *117 K for Argyra*, similar to or slightly cooler than Jupiter’s upper atmosphere.

### General Properties
Aurion is the more massive of the two co-orbital giants, with a mass of about 200 M🜨 (≈0.63 M♃) and a radius of ≈0.95 R♃. Its mean density is roughly 0.9 g/cm³, intermediate between Saturn and Jupiter. Surface gravity at the visible cloud decks is about 1.8 times Earth’s.

The planet rotates in roughly 11 hours, producing strong equatorial bulging and a system of zonal jets and banded cloud structures. Aurion’s Bond albedo of about 0.28 sets the fraction of incident starlight reflected. Residual interior heat adds to its thermal emission, which can be observed in the mid-infrared.

### Atmosphere and Appearance
Aurion’s atmosphere is dominated by molecular hydrogen and helium, with trace constituents and condensates shaping the observed colors. Key visible-wavelength species include:

- Ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium hydrosulfide (NH₄SH) cloud decks
- Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) and related sulfur species
- Phosphine (PH₃) and complex sulfur–phosphorus photolysis products
- Tholin-like complex organics in the upper hazes

These combine to produce a *golden–amber* palette. In reflected light:

- The *equatorial and mid-latitude belts* appear as layered *yellow-orange and ochre bands*, with subtle wave patterns and vortices.
- Higher-altitude zones are *paler and more yellow*, where fresh ammonia clouds overlie deeper sulfurous decks.
- Polar regions show slightly *darker, more bronze-brown* tones, with diffuse auroral hazes and filamentary structures.

The overall appearance is that of a warm, muted golden planet, similar in texture to a softly banded Jupiter but shifted toward deeper yellow and brass hues.

### Rings and Satellites
Aurion hosts a relatively faint, narrow ring system composed of dark, carbonaceous and silicate dust. The main ring zone extends to a few planetary radii. Material near and beyond the composition-dependent Roche limit forms replenished dusty structures and moonlet-associated arcs, visible in favorable forward-scattering geometries.

At least *three major inner moons* orbit within Aurion’s gravitational domain:

- They are comparable in size to large icy satellites, with mixed rock–ice compositions.
- Surfaces are dark, mantled by processed organics and thin volatile frosts.
- Tidal heating from Aurion’s gravity and mutual resonances sustains internal oceans or warm ice shells in some satellites.

Micrometeoroid impacts and sputtering from these moons are thought to replenish the faint rings over long timescales.

### General Properties
Argyra is the trailing member of the Trojan pair, with a mass of about 150 M🜨 (≈0.47 M♃) and a radius of ≈0.90 R♃. The resulting mean density is ~0.8 g/cm³, with a cloud-top gravity of about 1.5 g🜨. It rotates in roughly 12 hours, producing an oblate figure and broad, high-latitude jets.

Argyra’s higher Bond albedo (≈0.35) reflects more of the incident starlight than Aurion, yielding a slightly cooler equilibrium temperature despite the shared orbital distance.

### Atmosphere and Appearance
Argyra’s atmosphere is likewise hydrogen–helium dominated but supports colder, more reflective clouds:

- High-altitude *ammonia (NH₃) ice clouds*
- Underlying *water-ice and mixed condensate layers*
- Minor reflective particles whose composition remains under investigation. ZnS is a tentative candidate requiring a compatible condensation and transport model

These layers produce a visually *silvery-white* planet with subtle banding:

- *Broad, bright zones* give the disk a satin or brushed-metal look in reflected light.
- *Belts* appear as slightly *darker gray or bluish bands*, where cloud tops are thinner or deeper layers peek through.
- At high latitudes, thin hazes and auroral activity can create faint, icy glows above the cloud decks.

The overall impression is of a cool, pale giant with a smooth, metallic sheen, distinct from Aurion’s warmer, more sulfur-tinted tones.

### Rings and Satellites
Argyra is encircled by an extensive, *high-albedo ring system* extending several planetary radii from the equator. The rings are dominated by:

- *Water-ice particles* ranging from fine dust to larger ring fragments
- Bright mixed condensate grains and transient ammonia-bearing material supplied by the moons

These rings reflect a large fraction of incident starlight and appear bright even under the low flux at 4.1 AU. Variations in density and particle size generate sharply defined gaps and brightness asymmetries, possibly maintained by embedded moonlets.

Argyra hosts *at least four small icy moons*, orbiting just inside and outside the main ring region:

- Moons have high-albedo crusts of water and ammonia ice.
- Their resonances with the ring material sculpt edges and create density waves.
- Impacts and surface sputtering replenish the ring with fresh ice over time.

## Shared Dust and Resonant Structures

The combined gravity of Aurion and Argyra perturbs nearby small bodies, dust, and incoming comets. Potential structures include co-orbital debris concentrations and dust arcs shaped by resonances and radiation forces. Their exact locations and lifetimes need a dynamical model.

The **Silver Fringe** is a proposed interior 3:2 resonant population near 3.13 AU. Its defining feature is a Hilda-like triangular pattern. The two massive co-orbitals perturb the idealized single-giant pattern, so the persistence and shape of the three lobes remain modeling questions.

The pair scatters, captures, or redirects some bodies arriving from the Mirrorband. The resulting change in impact rates on the inner planets depends on the small-body distribution and orbital evolution.

## Modeling notes

The 4.1 AU orbit, masses, 60° ±30° separation, and contrasting colors define the pair. The triangular-equilibrium mass criterion is a preliminary stability check. Long-term stability of the complete system and the Silver Fringe, as well as any net impact shielding, remain unverified by numerical integration.

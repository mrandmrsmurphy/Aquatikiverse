# Stars

## Summary

A star is a ball of gas held together by gravity and powered by nuclear fusion. Almost everything about a main-sequence star follows from its **mass**: heavier stars are hotter, far brighter and shorter-lived. The [[Sun]] is the reference star, and the three stars of [[Thrírlitir]] show how the range works.

## The main sequence

During most of its life a star fuses hydrogen into helium in its core and sits on the *main sequence*. Its mass sets its core temperature, which sets the fusion rate and so the luminosity. For stars similar to the Sun, **L ∝ M^3.5** (roughly; the exponent is about 4 for heavier stars and 2.3 for the lightest). Since the fuel supply grows with M while the burn rate grows as M^3.5, **lifetime scales as M / L**, about 10 Gyr × (M/L) in solar units: a star of 1.3 M☉ and 2.5 L☉ lasts about 5 Gyr, while a 0.4 M☉ red dwarf with 0.04 L☉ lasts many tens of billions of years or more.

## Spectral types

| Type | Temperature | Typical mass | Color |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F | 6,000–7,500 K | 1.1–1.6 M☉ | yellow-white |
| G | 5,300–6,000 K | 0.9–1.1 M☉ | yellow (the Sun is G2) |
| K | 3,900–5,300 K | 0.6–0.9 M☉ | orange |
| M | 2,400–3,900 K | 0.08–0.6 M☉ | red |

A star's spectrum is close to a blackbody: its peak wavelength is λ_max = 2.898×10⁶ nm·K / T, so the Sun (5,772 K) peaks near 500 nm (green), a 3,800 K red dwarf near 760 nm and a 6,250 K F star near 460 nm. Hotter stars put proportionally more energy into ultraviolet, which matters for ozone and for life; cooler stars put more into the near-infrared.

Luminosity follows from radius and temperature: **L = 4πR²σT⁴**, or L/L☉ = (R/R☉)² (T/5,772 K)⁴. The [[Sun]] has R = 695,700 km and T = 5,772 K. Thrírlitir C (0.464 R☉, 3,800 K) gives 0.0405 L☉.

## Habitable zones

The distance at which a planet receives the same flux as Earth scales as √L, so a star's "Earth-like" distance is a ≈ √(L/L☉) AU. The conservative habitable zone is wider (its inner edge set by a runaway-greenhouse limit, its outer edge by the limit of CO₂ greenhouse warming) and shifts with the star's spectrum. Faint red dwarfs have close, narrow zones where tidal locking and flares matter; heavier F stars have wide, distant zones and shorter lifetimes.

## Binaries and flux variation

A close binary acts as one source of luminosity L₁ + L₂ for distant planets, but the flux varies with the binary's orbital phase. For a circumbinary planet at distance a the variation scales with (separation / a)² to leading order and is much larger for circumstellar orbits, where the companion's light arrives from a changing direction.

## In the Aquatikiverse

[[Thrírlitir]] is a hierarchical triple: A is an F8 V star (1.3 M☉, 1.35 R☉, 6,250 K, 2.505 L☉; M/L lifetime ≈ 5 Gyr), B a G8 V star (0.85 M☉, 0.88 R☉, 5,350 K, 0.566 L☉; ≈ 15 Gyr) and C an M1 V dwarf (0.40 M☉, 0.464 R☉, 3,800 K, 0.0405 L☉; ≈ 100 Gyr by this crude scaling). Their earth-like distances √L are 1.58 AU for A (where [[Aialon]] orbits) and 0.78 AU for the B+C pair (0.6065 L☉; where [[Valen]] orbits at 0.80 AU). A's more intense UV (UV-B 1.38 times the Sun's per unit flux) and B's red tail define the light environments of [[Aialon]], [[Valen]] and [[Aphrod]].

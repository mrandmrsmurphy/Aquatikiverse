**Azotia** is a cold, ammonia-dominated ocean planet orbiting the K4V star **Altmania B**, the secondary component of the [[Altmania System]]. It is the third known planet from its star, with a semi-major axis of 0.72 AU, and is notable for its extensive ammonia–water oceans, dense reducing atmosphere, and vibrant pink–purple sky generated by photochemical hazes.

Despite a mean surface temperature of around 260 K, Azotia maintains a chemically active environment conducive to exotic ammonia-adapted life. Its biosphere is built on photon-driven ammonia oxidation, methanogenesis, and anaerobic methane oxidation in cold, slushy seas.

## Physical Characteristics
### Orbit and rotation
Azotia orbits Altmania B at a semi-major axis of 0.72 AU with an orbital eccentricity of 0.10, yielding:

* Periastron distance ≈ 0.648 AU
* Apastron distance ≈ 0.792 AU

For a 0.70 M☉ star, Kepler’s Third Law gives an orbital period of about 0.73 Earth years, or roughly **267 Earth days**. Over one orbit, the incident stellar flux varies from about 0.48 F🜨 at periastron to 0.32 F🜨 at apastron, with a time-averaged value near 0.39 F🜨.

Azotia rotates once every 26 hours and is not tidally locked. The short day length and eccentric orbit together drive active weather patterns, with modest but noticeable seasonal shifts as the planet moves between periastron and apastron. The inclination is about 1° relative to the system reference plane; long-term stability additionally depends on planetary interactions, phases and stellar forcing.

### Mass, radius, and gravity
Azotia has:

* Mass: 2.0 M🜨
* Mean radius: 1.30 R🜨
* Mean density: ≈ 5.0 g/cm³
* Surface gravity: ≈ 1.18 g🜨

The density implies a largely rocky composition with a substantial silicate mantle and metallic core, plus moderate volatile enrichment. At fixed temperature and molar mass the enhanced gravity reduces the atmospheric scale height; pressures depend on column mass and structure but remains survivable for robust terrestrial-grade life or human visitors with augmentation.

The escape velocity, scaled from Earth, is approximately:

* **vₑₛc ≈ 14.0 km/s**

sufficient to retain a thick secondary atmosphere rich in ammonia and nitrogen only if photochemical loss, escape, condensate deposition and geological/biological replenishment balance over time.

Dissolved/particulate nitrate is accounted separately, not assigned a bulk atmospheric fraction. Water vapor, NH₃ and acid–base reaction products vary with ocean composition and temperature; CO₂ has strong dissolution/reaction sinks and is continually supplied near vents. The table is a working habitat reference, not a validated global atmosphere.

At 260 K pure ammonia has saturation pressure about **2.546 bar**. The proposed NH₃ partial pressure is about **1.748 bar**, requiring an effective liquid activity near **0.687** in a simple fugacity comparison. The pure-fluid comparison is therefore compatible with the chosen mixture at this temperature, while the actual NH₃–H₂O/salt mixture still needs a nonideal vapor-equilibrium calculation. No uncomputed ocean concentration is claimed to produce that activity automatically.

NH₃/CH₄ photochemistry produces organic/nitrogen-bearing haze. Pink–purple appearance remains the adopted visual design, with particle spectra and stellar scattering unmodeled. Ammonia–water rain, sleet, fog and slush redistribute volatiles; nitrogen is principally the carrier gas, not condensed pure N₂ at these temperatures. The old 3%-water and 1.6%-NOₓ/nitrate bins are retired.

The orbit still receives mean flux **0.38775 S⊕**. A reflective-cloud/haze and greenhouse model must yield the 260-K target; a 201-K equilibrium with a mere four-kelvin greenhouse increment would not reach it. The 260-K climate is not the result of a recent warming event or human terraforming history.

## Surface and Geology
### Oceans and ice caps
Azotia’s surface is dominated by global ammonia–water oceans. At surface pressures of 3 atm and low temperatures near 260 K, the oceans are not pure liquids but rather a spectrum of:

* Concentrated ammonia–water solutions
* Slushy, partially frozen mixtures
* Solid ammonia–water ices at higher latitudes and altitudes

Polar regions host extensive ammonia–water ice caps that expand and contract seasonally as insolation varies with orbital position. The oceans are moderately saline and highly conductive, enriched with dissolved minerals and gases leached from the crust and supplied by hydrothermal systems.

### Continents and landmasses
Several major continental masses and archipelagos rise above the global ocean:

* **Brassica** – The largest continent, characterized by iron-rich mountain belts and copper-bearing highlands, giving some regions a dull metallic sheen.
* **Virelia** – An equatorial archipelago of volcanically active islands and mid-ocean ridges, a hotspot for hydrothermal activity and localized warm currents.
* **Kessarine Basin** – A broad, shallow depression hosting sprawling ammonia marshlands and slush flats that seasonally freeze and thaw.

Geology ranges from silicate plateaus and basaltic plains to brassy copper-rich outcrops. Chemical weathering in ammonia–water solutions forms distinctive alteration minerals, including azotian analogs of clays and hydrated salts stable at low temperatures.

### Volcanism and tectonics
Azotia exhibits active plate tectonics and volcanism sustained by radiogenic silicate reservoirs, mantle/core cooling and a compatible convective interior. Key features include:

* Subduction zones driving mountain building and island arc volcanism.
* Mid-ocean spreading centers feeding hydrothermal vents.
* Cryovolcanic and mixed magmatic eruptions that release CO₂, H₂S, CH₄, and minor H₂.

Volcanic outgassing helps maintain atmospheric composition and injects redox-active species into both the air and oceans, supporting chemotrophic metabolisms in vent and basin environments.

## Climate
The global working mean is **260 K**, with principal open-sea habitats around **255–265 K** and colder coastal, upland and polar regions. Concentrated ammonia–water solutions support open seas; composition-dependent ice/slush develops in cold currents, sheltered basins and winter/polar zones. Some Slushcrawler habitats keep approximately 200–230-K regional conditions. Exact liquid fractions and temperatures require a climate/mixture model, not a claim that every concentration remains liquid.

Periastron forcing (about 0.476 S⊕) favors ice retreat, thicker clouds and convection; apastron forcing (about 0.319 S⊕) favors expansion, sleet/slush and inversions. Spin-axis orientation and thermal lag determine local seasonal outcomes; no obliquity is invented from the orbital eccentricity.

Ammonia–water rainstorms, sleet, droplet fog in N₂-rich carrier gas, powerful polar slush storms and long-lived ocean currents remain. Heat storage, cloud feedback and connected liquid refugia must support full reproductive/dormancy/migration cycles through the seasons. A stable habitat is the design priority; the particular pressure/mixture reference remains subject to that physical requirement.

## Magnetosphere
A partially molten iron core and active convection generate a modest but functional magnetic field. The magnetosphere:

* Deflects a substantial fraction of charged particles from Altmania B.
* Reduces non-thermal atmospheric escape, particularly of nitrogen and heavier gases.
* Does not completely prevent loss of light species such as hydrogen, contributing to a slow, long-term leakage of H₂ to space.

## Chemical Environment and Hydrogen Sources
Azotia’s crust is rich in iron and copper, with notable levels of sulfur, aluminum, and silicon. Trace elements such as molybdenum, vanadium, and nickel are crucial cofactors in Azotian biochemistry.

Beyond biological ammonia oxidation, additional hydrogen is supplied by geochemical processes, notably:

* **Serpentinization and water–rock reactions** – When ammonia–water fluids interact with ultramafic rocks in the upper mantle and crust, Fe²⁺-bearing minerals are oxidized, producing molecular hydrogen:

  `FeO-rich silicates + H₂O → oxidized iron minerals + H₂`

These reactions occur along fracture zones, mid-ocean ridges, and deep fault networks, feeding H₂-rich fluids into hydrothermal systems. The resulting hydrogen flux supports methanogens and other chemotrophs, augmenting the biologically generated H₂ pool and helping buffer the biosphere against hydrogen loss to space.

## Potential for Life
Main article: [[Life on Azotia]].

Azotia’s cold but chemically active conditions are well suited to exotic, ammonia-adapted life:

* Dense NH₃–N₂ atmosphere and 3 atm surface pressure support the intended ammonia–water liquid/slush habitats subject to the mixture and vapor-equilibrium model.
* A strong, tightly coupled H–C–N redox network (ammonia oxidation, methanogenesis, anaerobic methane oxidation) sustains primary productivity.
* Geothermal and serpentinization-driven hydrogen sources provide additional energy in deep and coastal environments.

The biosphere is thought to include:

* Ammonia-oxidizing phototrophs (Ammonioplankton) in upper oceans.
* Methanogens and anaerobic methane oxidizers in deeper waters and sediments.
* Slush-adapted macrofauna and microbial mats in coastal marshes and vent fields.

Over billions of years, these systems have created a stable, self-regulating biosphere that keeps ammonia, methane, and hydrogen in dynamic balance despite continuous external forcing and slow atmospheric escape.

## Physical basis

The normalized local atmospheric reference is NH₃ 57.5%, N₂ 39%, CH₄ 2.5%, H₂ 0.9%, H₂S 0.05%, water vapor 0.03%, CO₂ 0.005% and other minor gases 0.015%, with nitrates mainly dissolved or particulate and only trace gas-phase NOₓ; water, CO₂ and ammonia vary with condensation, reactions and vent input. The NIST Antoine coefficients for 239.6–371.5 K ([NIST NH₃](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C7664417&Mask=4); [NIST N₂](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C7727379&Mask=4)) give a pure-NH₃ saturation pressure of 2.546 bar at 260 K, and 57.5% of 3 atm is 1.748 bar, which requires an effective liquid ammonia activity near 0.687 in a simple vapor and fugacity comparison. This does not establish an ocean concentration: a nonideal NH₃–H₂O/salt model must supply the activity and a stable liquid domain, with the reduced water partial pressure needing the same validation ([experimental ammonia–water phase work](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19940031629/downloads/19940031629.pdf)). No pure N₂ fog is claimed; NH₃/H₂O droplets and ice are carried in the gas. At a mean flux of 0.38775 S⊕ and e = 0.10, a radiative-convective cloud, haze and greenhouse model must yield the 260-K target, and trace-gas opacity, scattering, NH₃ photolysis and ammonia and CO₂ salts remain physical model requirements.

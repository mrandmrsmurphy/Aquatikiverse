**Gigafusc** is a massive, warm T-type brown dwarf orbiting the ultracool red dwarf [[Stellula]] at 0.1000 AU. With a mass of 14.0 M♃ and a radius about 1.1 times that of Jupiter, it is below the hydrogen-burning limit and is the most massive bound object in the [[Stellula System]] apart from Stellula itself. Gigafusc completes an orbit every 37.80 days and resides well outside the orbits of [[Tito]], [[Harth]] and [[Virans]].

Internally heated and rotating rapidly once every 4.0000 hours, Gigafusc is visibly oblate and hosts a deep, banded atmosphere, intense aurorae and a bright, multi-component ring system of rock, sulfur and dust. It is accompanied by two major moons, the volcanic Scauron and the ocean-bearing Lumora, plus a retinue of smaller moonlets on equatorial and highly inclined orbits.

## Orbit and Hill sphere

Gigafusc orbits Stellula at a nearly circular distance of 0.1000 AU with a period of 37.80 days, traveling at an orbital velocity of 28.8 km/s relative to Stellula. The orbit is close to coplanar with those of the inner planets, making conjunctions and occasional occultations behind Stellula possible when viewed from [[Tito]] and [[Harth]].

The nominal small-companion Hill formula gives a scale of ~0.038 AU (~5.7 million km), with limited precision for this mass ratio. The circular coplanar test-particle fit gives a prograde circum-Gigafusc screening distance of ~2.07 million km. Scauron and Lumora lie inside it. Some irregular moonlets extend beyond it, and their stability depends on inclination, eccentricity, and direction of motion. See [[Stellula System#Global dynamical stability]].

From Harth at favorable opposition, Gigafusc attains an angular diameter of about 0.68°, somewhat larger than Earth's Moon as seen from Earth but much darker in reflected light.

## Physical characteristics

Gigafusc's mass of ~14.0 M♃ combined with a radius of ~1.1 R♃ implies a mean density of about 13.0 g/cm³, far greater than that of gas giants like Jupiter. This high density reflects the partial electron degeneracy of its interior and the weak dependence of radius on mass in the brown dwarf regime.

Surface gravity at the nominal 1-bar level is around 286.8 m/s² (~29.2 g). Despite this enormous gravity, the rapid 4-hour rotation produces a measurable equatorial bulge: the equatorial radius exceeds the polar radius by roughly 4 %, resulting in an observable flattening (oblateness) of ~0.04. The equatorial rotation speed, ~34.9 km/s, remains well below the local breakup velocity but is sufficient to organize the atmosphere into strong zonal jets.

The brown dwarf radiates primarily due to residual heat from formation and ongoing contraction. External irradiation from Stellula contributes only a minor fraction of the energy budget, corresponding to an equilibrium temperature of ~96 K for zero Bond albedo and uniform redistribution at 0.1000 AU (19.7 W/m² against the 13.6 kW/m² the photosphere radiates). A 14 M♃ brown dwarf at the system's 2 Gyr age has a photospheric temperature of about 650–750 K, so ~700 K is adopted (cooler 350–400 K objects are older or lighter). That gives a luminosity of about 1.1×10²¹ W, 2% of Stellula's, emerging in the near and mid-infrared with the strongest emission in the 1–1.3 µm window of methane-dominated T dwarfs. The radius of 1.1 R♃ is at the upper end of what an aged brown dwarf of this mass has (1.0–1.1 R♃) and is adopted.

## Atmosphere and weather

Gigafusc's atmosphere is several tens of thousands of kilometers deep and is composed mainly of hydrogen and helium, with methane (dominant among the carbon carriers at 700 K), water vapor, ammonia, a little carbon monoxide, and broad sodium and potassium absorption. At a photospheric temperature near 700 K the upper atmosphere is too warm for water or ammonia ice to condense. The condensate clouds are deeper: sulfide and salt clouds (Na₂S, KCl, ZnS) form well below the photosphere, and patchy holes in them let brighter light from deeper, hotter layers escape.

The rapid rotation and strong internal heat flux drive a system of powerful zonal jets and long-lived vortices:

- Broad, alternating east–west jets encircle the brown dwarf at multiple latitudes.
- Dark belts and slightly brighter zones band the atmosphere, subtler than Jupiter's, with brightness variations tied to the patchy deep cloud.
- Long-lived cyclonic storms form at jet boundaries, some persisting for years.
- Episodic "great storms" – bright convective eruptions that clear the deep cloud – appear as pale copper or grey ovals and slowly drift with the local wind.

Lightning discharges occur within convective towers, producing brief white-blue flashes visible on the nightside in high-resolution imagery. These storms inject ionized material into the magnetosphere and contribute to transient enhancements in Gigafusc's auroral activity.

In reflected optical light, Gigafusc appears as a very dark, slightly purple-brown disk with faint banding (the sodium and potassium absorption gives T dwarfs a magenta cast), while in the near-infrared it is much brighter than in the visible. The nightside is almost entirely black except for lightning flashes and polar auroral glows.

## Magnetosphere and aurorae

Gigafusc likely possesses a powerful internal dynamo, generating a magnetic field significantly stronger than Jupiter's. Its magnetosphere extends well beyond the orbits of its major moons and into the inner regions of the Stellula System, where it interacts with the stellar wind from Stellula.

Key features of the magnetosphere include:

- A dense plasma torus sourced primarily by volcanic activity on the inner moon [[#Scauron|Scauron]], rich in sulfur, oxygen and sodium ions.
- Strong field-aligned currents connecting the brown dwarf's ionosphere to its moons, analogous to the Io–Jupiter interaction but on a larger scale.
- A vast magnetodisk of charged particles co-rotating with Gigafusc and slowly diffusing outward.

Aurorae appear at both poles as:

- Bright, thick ovals encircling the magnetic poles, emitting across ultraviolet, visible and infrared wavelengths.
- Curtains, spirals and arcs that vary on timescales from minutes to hours, modulated by interactions with Scauron's plasma torus and by changes in the stellar wind.
- Occasional "auroral storms" during which the polar regions glow so intensely that, as seen from Tito's nightside, Gigafusc briefly brightens by a factor of several in visible light.

From Titonian cities on Tito's dark hemisphere, Gigafusc is visible as a charcoal disk crowned by green-blue and violet auroral rings, sometimes faintly luminous even when Stellula is below the horizon.

## Rings

Gigafusc hosts a bright, multi-component ring system, among the most optically prominent structures in the Stellula System. The rings are maintained by a combination of collisional grinding, volcanic ejecta and shepherding by small moonlets.

Ring particles near a 700 K brown dwarf are warm: their equilibrium temperature is about 350 K at 160,000 km, 280 K at 250,000 km and 220 K at 400,000 km, and water ice is stable in a vacuum only below about 150 K. The rings are therefore made of rock, sulfur and salt dust, not water ice, and water ice survives only beyond about 1.3 million km (Lumora's region and farther).

The main elements are:

- **Inner dust ring** – A narrow, dense ring from ~120,000 to 180,000 km from Gigafusc's center, composed of dark, charged dust and sulfur-rich particles that drift inward from Scauron's plasma torus and from small moonlets.

- **Shepherded A-ring** – A brighter ring centered near ~160,000 km, confined by two small shepherd moonlets, *Anker* and *Brim* (radii ~60 km), which orbit slightly inward and outward of the ring’s core. Their gravitational perturbations create scalloped edges, wave patterns and narrow gaps. Fresh sulfur and salt coatings make the particles bright.

- **Diffuse B-ring** – A broad, semi-transparent ring extending roughly from ~250,000 to 400,000 km, fed by fine dust and salt grains expelled from Scauron and by micrometeorite impacts on the inner moonlets. This ring is bright in forward-scattered light and in the infrared.

- **Faint halo** – At higher altitudes above the equatorial plane and out to ~800,000 km, a tenuous cloud of dust and plasma forms a halo around the brown dwarf, detectable mainly by its influence on radio emissions and as a slight forward-scattering enhancement during favorable alignments.

In telescopic views from Tito and Harth, the rings appear as thin, bright lines slicing across Gigafusc's dark disk near equinox configurations, and as flattened ellipses offset from the limb when viewed obliquely.

## Moons and moonlets

Gigafusc is accompanied by two major moons, a handful of mid-sized regular satellites embedded in the ring system, and a cloud of small irregular moonlets on inclined and sometimes retrograde orbits.

#### Scauron

Scauron is a dense, rocky moon orbiting at ~945,000 km with a period of 1.5862 days, exactly half of Lumora's 3.1724 days. This 2:1 resonance (like Io and Europa) holds Scauron's orbit slightly eccentric (e ≈ 0.003 gives ~0.9 W/m² of tidal heating, about half of Io's), driving continuous volcanism. Without the resonance its eccentricity would damp in a few thousand years; with it the damping time of ~0.5 Myr is replenished by Lumora's pull. From Scauron, Gigafusc spans ~9.5° of sky.

- Hundreds of active calderas dot its surface, emitting silicate lava and sulfur-rich gases.
- Volcanic plumes extend hundreds of kilometers above the surface, ejecting dust and ions into the surrounding space.
- Ejecta escaping Scauron's gravity supply material to the plasma torus co-rotating with Gigafusc and, as dust drifts inward, to the inner rings.

In infrared imagery, Scauron’s nightside glows with the red-orange light of lava lakes and hot flows, making it a significant contributor to the small-scale structure of Gigafusc's thermal emission map.

#### Lumora

Lumora orbits at ~1,500,000 km with a period of 3.1724 days. It is a large, ice-rich moon with a differentiated interior:

- A rocky core overlain by a thick mantle of high-pressure ice.
- A global subsurface liquid ocean, kept warm by tidal heating (0.01–0.1 W/m² for e of 0.001–0.003, from the resonance with Scauron) and radioactive decay.
- A thin nitrogen–methane atmosphere with wispy, high-altitude clouds and occasional hazes.

From Lumora’s surface, Gigafusc appears as a gigantic, banded disk about 6.0° across, ringed and crowned by aurora. Stellula delivers about 19.6 W/m² at normal incidence and Gigafusc's 700 K infrared adds about 37 W/m², nearly twice as much, so Gigafusc supplies most of the incident power and sets an equilibrium temperature of about 105–115 K (a thin N₂/CH₄ atmosphere is stable there). Gigafusc also dominates the apparent sky area and provides reflected light and auroral glows. Lumora is the most plausible site for future outposts in the Gigafuscan system.

### Ringside moonlets and mid-sized satellites

Between the ring system and Lumora lie several mid-sized regular satellites and numerous small moonlets:

- Three principal equatorial moonlets (typical radii 100–300 km) orbit between ~550,000 and 850,000 km, inside Scauron. These bodies, informally grouped as the "Family Moons," are heavily cratered and rock-rich (at 150–190 K their surfaces cannot keep water ice, which survives only below the surface) and are likely fragments of earlier, larger satellites disrupted by impacts or resonant interactions.
- Dozens of smaller moonlets, from a few kilometers to tens of kilometers across, inhabit the gaps and edges of the rings. Their gravitational influence sculpts narrow ringlets and wakes, and collisions among them continually resupply fine dust.

### Outer irregular cluster

Across and beyond the nominal prograde screening limit, between ~1.8×10^6 and 2.5×10^6 km, orbits a swarm of small irregular moonlets:

- Typical radii range from 10 to 80 km.
- Orbits are significantly inclined (40°–150°) and moderately eccentric.
- Several members are on retrograde paths relative to Gigafusc's rotation.

These outer bodies, which lie where Gigafusc's own heat falls to ~100 K, are thought to be captured planetesimals or fragments from past collisions. Collisions within the population may supply a very faint outer dust component to Gigafusc's extended halo.

## Appearance from the inner system

From the inner planets, Gigafusc is a distinctive, if faint, presence:

- From [[Harth]], at favorable opposition and closest approach, Gigafusc appears as a dark, slightly brown disk about 0.68° across, smaller and dimmer than the star but large enough that its shape is clearly resolved with modest telescopes.
- During rare geometries when Gigafusc passes behind Stellula as seen from Harth, Stellula can occult the smaller apparent disc completely at a central alignment. Rings or auroral light may be seen near the stellar limb before or after occultation.
- From Tito's nightside, Gigafusc is more often seen against a dark sky, with its auroral ovals visible as faint, colored glows at high latitudes, sometimes bright enough to be perceived unaided under optimal conditions.

In long-duration photometric observations, Gigafusc's rotation and weather imprint periodic and stochastic variations on its light curve, as banded clouds, storms, rings and bright moon transits move in and out of view.

## Role in Stellula System culture

Gigafusc functions as:

- A distant, imposing backdrop for the more densely inhabited regions around Stellula and Tito.
- A natural laboratory for high-energy plasma physics, magnetospheric science and comparative meteorology of brown dwarfs.
- A cultural symbol of power, darkness and hidden light for inhabitants of Tito and, in later eras, Harth. Its vast auroral crowns and enormous disk in Lumora's sky lend themselves to mythic interpretations as a "black lantern" or "shadow sun."

Although no permanent human colonies exist in the Gigafuscan system at the current epoch, robotic probes and occasional crewed flybys have mapped its rings, moons and aurorae in detail, making Gigafusc one of the most scientifically characterized brown dwarfs in the known Aquatikiverse.

## Physical basis

**Orbit and spin.** The 0.1000 AU relative orbit gives a period of about 37.80 days and a relative speed of about 28.8 km/s using the combined mass of Stellula and Gigafusc; the 4-hour rotation and ~80,000 km equatorial radius give an equatorial speed of about 34.9 km/s.

**Temperature and luminosity.** For 14 M♃ at 1.1 R♃ (13.0 g/cm³), evolutionary models give an effective temperature of about 650–750 K at 2 Gyr (cited from memory, not recomputed). The adopted ~700 K gives a luminosity of 1.06×10²¹ W, 1.9% of Stellula's. At Lumora's distance (1.5×10⁶ km) Gigafusc's infrared flux is 37.4 W/m² against 19.6 W/m² from the star, which gives an equilibrium temperature of about 105–115 K there.

**Rings and clouds.** A ring particle's equilibrium temperature is T = T_eff (R / 2d)^½:

| Distance from Gigafusc (km) | Particle temperature (K) |
| --- | --- |
| 160,000 | 350 |
| 250,000 | 280 |
| 400,000 | 220 |

Water ice needs less than about 150 K, so the main rings are rock, sulfur and salt dust, and ice survives only beyond about 1.3×10⁶ km. At 700 K the upper atmosphere has no water or ammonia ice clouds; Na₂S, KCl and ZnS clouds lie below the photosphere.

**Scauron and Lumora.** Scauron orbits at 944,941 km (1.5862 d) in a 2:1 resonance with Lumora (1.4×10²³ kg, four times Scauron's 3.1×10²² kg). Scauron's eccentricity damps in 0.53 Myr, so volcanism there depends on the resonance forcing it. Tidal heating on Scauron is 0.10, 0.94 and 2.6 W/m² for e = 10⁻³, 0.003 and 5×10⁻³ (k₂/Q = 0.015). Lumora's heating is 0.014, 0.125 and 1.39 W/m² for e = 10⁻³, 3×10⁻³ and 10⁻² (k₂/Q = 0.01). Gigafusc spans 9.5° from Scauron and 6.0° from Lumora. The Family Moons orbit at 550,000–850,000 km; they are rock-rich and their surfaces are at 150–190 K.

**Adopted values.** The 1.1 R♃ radius is adopted. Tidal heating figures assume Io-like k₂/Q values and are scalings, not integrations. Ring particle temperatures are equilibrium estimates; auroral and ring brightness are qualitative. The outer irregulars' long-term stability remains an open modeling detail.

**Stellula** is an ultracool red dwarf star of spectral type M9.5 V in the constellation [Monoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoceros), about 12 parsecs (39 light-years) from the Sun. It has a mass of approximately 0.0800 M☉ and a radius of about 0.1000 R☉, placing it near the theoretical lower mass limit for sustained hydrogen fusion. Stellula is the central star of the compact [[Stellula System]], which includes three known planets and a brown dwarf companion, all orbiting within 0.1000 AU.

## Stellar characteristics

Stellula is one of the smallest and faintest main-sequence stars capable of long-term hydrogen burning. With a total luminosity of about 0.000145 L☉, it emits only ~0.0145 % of the Sun's energy output. The star's effective temperature is estimated at roughly 2000 K, concentrating most of its radiation in the deep red and near-infrared. In visible light, Stellula is extremely dim; its apparent magnitude of 19.9 places it far beyond naked-eye visibility, requiring moderately large amateur or professional telescopes to detect.

The combination of 0.0800 M☉ and 0.1000 R☉ implies a surface gravity about eight times that of the Sun. In logarithmic units, this corresponds to a photospheric gravity of log *g* ≈ 5.3 (cgs), typical for ultracool dwarfs near the bottom of the main sequence. The interior is expected to be fully convective, with no radiative core, leading to efficient mixing of material throughout the star.

Metallicity for Stellula is assumed to be near solar, consistent with its role as a host to a compact, metal-rich planetary system. Detailed spectroscopic abundances have not been specified in published catalogues within the Aquatikiverse setting, but its high planet-to-star mass ratio suggests a protoplanetary disc enriched in heavy elements.

### Lifetime and evolution

Low-mass red dwarfs like Stellula evolve extremely slowly. A rough main-sequence lifetime can be estimated from the ratio of stellar mass to luminosity:

$$
t_\mathrm{MS} \sim 10^{10}~\text{yr} \times \frac{M_\star / M_\odot}{L_\star / L_\odot}.
$$

For Stellula, with *M* ≈ 0.0800 M☉ and *L* ≈ 0.000145 L☉, this gives

$$
t_\mathrm{MS} \sim 10^{10}~\text{yr} \times \frac{0.0800}{0.000145} \approx 5\times10^{12}~\text{yr},
$$

or on the order of 5 trillion years. At an adopted age of ~2 billion years for the Stellula System, the star is still in an early phase of its main-sequence evolution, with negligible structural changes expected over many times the current age of the universe.

Like many ultracool dwarfs, Stellula may exhibit magnetic activity and stellar flares, particularly at higher energies in the ultraviolet and X-ray bands. The adopted Stellula is quiet, with an X-ray output of about 10⁻⁶ of its bolometric luminosity. The level of activity matters because Harth is at 0.012034 AU: an energy-limited escape estimate (10% efficiency) removes Harth's 1 atm atmosphere in about 6, 60 or 600 Myr for X-ray fractions of 10⁻⁴, 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁶, and Tito's 1.7 atm in 17, 170 or 1,700 Myr. The adopted quiet value and a heavy-gas escape efficiency of a few percent let the atmospheres last the system's age, with volcanic and biological resupply; this is an assumption.

## Location and visibility

Stellula lies in the constellation [Monoceros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoceros) at approximate J2000 coordinates:

- Right ascension: 07<sup>h</sup> 12<sup>m</sup> 30.0<sup>s</sup>  
- Declination: −09° 05′ 00″  

At a distance of 12.0 parsecs, its absolute visual magnitude of 19.5 translates to an apparent magnitude of about 19.9. This makes Stellula far too faint to be seen without a telescope. In optical surveys, it appears as a very faint, extremely red point source; in near-infrared bands, the star is relatively brighter and easier to detect.

Because of its proximity, Stellula would likely exhibit a measurable proper motion across the sky over decade timescales, similar to many nearby red dwarfs. In fictional observational catalogues, it may appear in high-proper-motion lists and infrared surveys dedicated to the search for ultracool dwarfs and brown dwarfs in the Solar neighbourhood.

## Planetary system

Main article: [[Stellula System]]

Stellula hosts a remarkably compact planetary system whose major orbits extend to 0.1000 AU from the star. The system consists of three planets and a brown dwarf companion:

- [[Tito]] – a water-rich 10.2 M⊕ super-Earth (2.8 R⊕) at 0.009184 AU, tidally locked and in the adopted near-3:2 configuration with Harth.
- [[Harth]] – an approximately Earth-mass, Earth-radius planet at 0.012034 AU, receiving ~1 S⊕ from Stellula and occupying the center of the system's habitable zone. Harth is tidally locked and subject to frequent deep eclipses by Tito.
- [[Virans]] – a ~13 M⊕ mini-Neptune at 0.0330 AU, lying just beyond the system's frost line and retaining a methane-rich volatile envelope.
- [[Gigafusc]] – a ~14 M♃ warm T-type brown dwarf (~700 K) at 0.1000 AU, physically larger than Stellula but orbiting at a much greater distance, with its own system of moons.

The star's low luminosity and the resulting close-in habitable zone are central to the system's architecture. Harth orbits at a distance where the stellar flux is essentially equal to that received by Earth from the Sun, despite being only about 1.8 million kilometres from Stellula. The compact configuration leads to pronounced gravitational interactions and resonances, as well as a rich pattern of mutual eclipses and transits when viewed from within the system.

## Angular size and illumination

Because the planets orbit Stellula at very small distances, the star appears large in their skies. For example, at Harth's orbit (0.012034 AU), Stellula's apparent angular diameter is roughly 4.4°, about eight times that of the Sun as seen from Earth. Tito’s slightly larger apparent disc (4.79° against 4.43°) at inferior conjunction allows the super-Earth Tito to fully cover the stellar disc when its orbit carries it directly in front of Stellula as seen from Harth, producing deep and recurrent stellar eclipses.

The star's red-infrared spectral energy distribution means that planetary surfaces and atmospheres around Stellula will be illuminated primarily by long-wavelength light: at Harth only 0.8% of the star's power falls in 400–700 nm and 9.3% in 700–1,100 nm, giving photon fluxes of about 56 and 1,000 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (2.4% and 36% of Earth's). Harth’s native and engineered photosynthetic lineages therefore use far-red pigments. At the orbit of Virans, the incident flux drops to about 0.133 S⊕, corresponding to an equilibrium temperature in the mid-100 K range and favouring the retention of thick volatile atmospheres and ices.

## Physical basis

**Luminosity and temperature.** L = 4πR²σT⁴ gives 1.44×10⁻⁴ L☉ for R = 0.10 R☉ and T = 2,000 K; the adopted 0.000145 L☉ gives 2,003 K. This lies within the range for an M9.5 dwarf near the hydrogen-burning limit, and evolutionary models (cited from memory) give 2,000–2,300 K at 2 Gyr. M9.5 V and hydrogen burning remain setting classifications.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| 10⁻⁴ | 0.135 | 6 | 17 |

Earth receives about 0.0046 W/m². The system's ages therefore require a quiet star: Stellula's X-ray output is taken as about 10⁻⁶ of its luminosity, with a heavy-gas escape efficiency of a few percent and volcanic and biological resupply. This quiet-star level is an adopted assumption, as is the activity level of the flare record.

**Cryodon** is a cold, massive ice giant orbiting the circumbinary star system [[Aethonix]] at a distance of 6.0 AU, in the outer [[Aethonix System]]. It is among the most distant major bodies discovered in the system and one of the most massive, with a bulk mass of approximately 17 Earth masses and a radius four times that of Earth. It orbits beyond the habitable zone in a nearly circular, equatorial orbit, completing one revolution in roughly 11 Earth years.

## Classification and Orbit
Cryodon is classified as a super-Earth-mass ice giant, similar in composition to Uranus or Neptune at roughly Neptune-class mass and gravity (~10.4 m/s²). It orbits the [[Aethonix]] binary stars—a G0V and K4V pair separated by 0.15 AU—in a P-type (circumbinary) configuration, receiving only ~4.7% of Earth’s solar flux. Its orbital inclination is approximately 1°, with negligible eccentricity.

## Physical Characteristics
Cryodon's mean density of 1.46 g/cm³ is consistent with a family of H₂/He, high-pressure volatile-rich and rocky/metallic interiors; it does not uniquely determine core fraction or the state of the volatile layers. Its equilibrium irradiation-equilibrium reference is ~123 K (≈ −150 °C), consistent with its distance, low Bond albedo (~0.2), and reduced stellar flux. These conditions contribute to its deep blue to cyan atmospheric coloration.

The atmosphere is primarily H₂/He with 2–3% methane, responsible for strong absorption in red and infrared bands. Additional minor species include acetylene (C₂H₂) in the upper atmosphere and suspected traces of phosphine (PH₃) and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) at depth. The planet's appearance is dominated by high-latitude hazes and mid-latitude banding, with yellow-green zones and reddish-grey transient clouds breaking the otherwise cool palette.

## Ring System
Cryodon hosts a complex, dark ring system composed of carbonaceous dust, silicates, and micron-sized debris:

- **Inner Dust Ring** (strand centred near 1.6R<sub>C</sub>, within the broad1.5–1.7R<sub>C</sub>location zone): ~200 km strand width; sourced from erosion of inner moonlets.
- **Arc Ring Alpha** (~1.9 R<sub>C</sub>): ~50 km wide (discontinuous); silicate-rich clumps, maintained by shepherd moons.
- **Mid Ring** (strand centred near 2.05R<sub>C</sub>, within the broad2.0–2.2R<sub>C</sub>location zone): ~300 km strand width; composed of dark ice and silicate grains.
- **Outer Debris Sheet** (2.1–2.3 R<sub>C</sub>): a diffuse boundary zone between rings and major moons.

These rings are much fainter than Saturn’s and more akin to those of Jupiter or Neptune in structure and reflectivity.

## Moons
Cryodon is orbited by a complex satellite system comprising both major and minor moons.

### Major Moons
The adopted census distinguishes six named major moons. The following kilometre sizes are **radii**, an explicit convention chosen for this reference:

- **Evara** (~3000 km): tectonic highland world with cratered basins; thin N₂/CH₄ frost haze.
- **Halix** (~2200 km): likely ocean moon with subsurface water and active plumes.
- **Vesca** (~1800 km): cryovolcanic; tiger-striped rifts and episodic phosphine-associated plume/haze chemistry, with the actual aerosol chromophore unmodelled.
- **Zhenya** (~1500 km): hydrocarbon-cycle analogue with mixed/ethane-rich liquids and dense pink-orange haze; exact atmospheric inventory and local phases require a thermal model.
- **Roqan** (~1200 km): captured irregular on a retrograde, eccentric orbit.
- **Myrrh** (~900 km): tidally flexed with cryolava fissures and NH₃/N₂ emissions.

### Minor Moons
Cryodon also has 50–200 minor moons, ranging from a few kilometers to ~800 km. These include:

- **Shepherd moons** (6–12): confining narrow rings.
- **Captured asteroids** (50–100): eccentric, chaotic trajectories.
- **Fragment chains** (20–30): likely remnants of collisions or tidal stripping.
- **Quasi-hydrostatic icy moons** (10–15): 300–800 km, may retain volatiles.

Most minor moons lack atmospheres and have irregular shapes.

## Observation and Discovery
Cryodon was discovered in 2294 by the Aethonix Survey Consortium during a thermal and visible-light imaging campaign conducted by the Carina Deep Array. Its direct detection was facilitated by its large size, cool temperature, and strong methane absorption features. Its atmospheric and ring features were further resolved through follow-up occultation and spectral analyses.

# Aethonix System

![[images/Aethonix_system_diagram.svg]]

*Schematic layout of the Aethonix planetary system (without AB)*

The **Aethonix System** is the natural G0V/K4V circumbinary system of [[Aethonix]], in Vela near Carina, about 12.9pc (~42light-years) from Earth in the Orion Arm. Eight planets, three temperate terrestrial societies, an industrial super-Earth and the complex Lunaran biosphere share the system. The adopted reference age is4 Gyr and the common modern setting epoch is year 3000; historical dates remain attached to their original events. No Lytherin-style manufacture or heating swarm is assumed.

## Asteroid Belt
The primary asteroid belt of the Aethonix System spans from approximately 2.0 to 3.5 astronomical units, in circumbinary orbit beyond the main terrestrial planets and partly overlapping the outer portion of the nominal classical HZ. It is a complex, ancient structure believed to be the fragmented residue of early planet formation, dynamically sculpted by the influence of both the central binary stars and the system’s outer giant planets. The belt is divided into three primary regions—inner, central, and outer—each exhibiting distinct compositional, spectral, and dynamical characteristics.

The belt's visual appearance is subtle but discernible from several of the inhabited worlds, particularly under high-resolution telescopic observation or long-exposure imaging. Its architecture is marked by overlapping collisional families, embedded resonant minor-body groups, and anomalously large planetary remnants, giving it both scientific and aesthetic significance.

### Inner Belt (2.0–2.4 AU)
The inner asteroid belt is dominated by S-type asteroids, silicate-rich and moderately reflective, with typical geometric albedos in the range of 0.20–0.25. Their surfaces are composed primarily of olivine and pyroxene, with occasional metallic inclusions, suggesting partial differentiation during the earliest epochs of the system’s accretion history.

This region is the most densely populated and displays several well-defined collisional families, likely originating from a series of hypervelocity impacts during the late heavy bombardment phase. Some fragments are compositionally homogeneous, while others exhibit spectral layering—evidence of shattered protoplanetary bodies with internal structure.

From Goku or Velora, its dust can contribute a faint zodiacal-light-like glow; long exposures and enhanced survey maps distinguish a pale inner arc. Future amateur observatories can track selected minor bodies, but their appearance and detectability depend on flux/phase/instrumentation rather than a resolved naked-eye10-kmdisk.

The resonant families called the "Dawn Lanterns" are assigned near Rimeas’s exterior3:2location (~2.264 AU). They are not trojans60°ahead/behind an empty orbit: true trojans are1:1coorbitals of an identified host. Their phase occupancy and survival remain to be tested. They are enriched in metallic S-type fragments and show anomalously high rotation rates, hinting at tidal interaction or prior collision history.

### Central Belt (2.4–3.0 AU)
The central zone is notably darker and more compositionally primitive. Dominated by C-type (carbonaceous) asteroids, this region has a low average albedo (~0.04) and is rich in hydrated silicates, clay minerals, and complex organic compounds. Many of these bodies are thought to have never undergone significant heating, preserving the volatile inventory of the system’s early solar nebula.

Perhaps the most scientifically and mythologically intriguing feature of the central belt is the presence of the so-called “Ghost Planet” remnants—large, partially differentiated bodies, likely the remains of a terrestrial-class protoplanet that failed to form or was catastrophically disrupted. These include:

- **The Golden Heart** – a 160 km fragment of exposed iron sulfide core, visibly yellow–bronze in albedo-enhanced imagery.
- **Shattered Thalos** – a dispersed swarm of green basaltic fragments, likely crustal remnants rich in olivine.
- **The Violet Tooth** – a monolithic, high-albedo shard laced with zircon and rare feldspars, exhibiting iridescent diffraction patterns under spectrometry.

Several of these objects are believed to be parent bodies for smaller collisional families extending through the entire mid-belt. Their orbits are moderately inclined and exhibit low eccentricity, consistent with early collisional origin rather than recent disruption.

In enhanced telescopic maps, the central belt appears darker and more broken, with mapped gaps and reflectance structure rather than distant asteroids casting visible silhouettes across an empty sky. It is often described poetically as the “shadow river” dividing the bright and burning bands.

### Outer Belt (3.0–3.5 AU)
The outer belt transitions into a domain dominated by D-type asteroids and extinct comet nuclei. These bodies are reddish or amber in coloration, with albedos typically ranging from 0.05 to 0.15. Their compositions include tholins, amorphous carbon, frozen ammonia clays, and potentially unstable hydrated organics. Many retain partially devolatilized crusts and may once have exhibited transient comae.

Despite their dark primary reflectance, the pervasive dust and fine ice grains in this region scatter sunlight strongly at oblique angles. In favorable long-exposure/enhanced observations, the dust can give the mapped belt a copper-tinged band—especially striking from the high latitudes of Rimeas, where the sky is otherwise cold and dark.

Embedded within the outer belt are populations of extinct cometary bodies: dark, pockmarked nuclei whose volatiles have long since sublimated. Their low albedos create dark survey-map regions, not literal broad shadows cast across a uniformly luminous ring.

The red–orange groups mapped near~3.3 AU are assigned to Noctilux’s interior4:3resonance region (~3.302 AU), rather than mislabeled as trojans. This identifies a possible dynamical association, not a completed resonance-occupancy simulation. They are rich in D-type organics and contain the densest known aggregation of tholins in the system. Infrared observations suggest that some bodies may still contain pockets of frozen gases buried beneath regolith layers.

Size-dependent transport includes Yarkovsky drift for suitable larger rotating bodies and Poynting–Robertson drag for small grains. Dust grains spiraling inward create faint radial streaks best seen in thermal IR bands, providing the poetic “invisible rain” imagery without a universal resolved radial-stream morphology.

### Observational Significance
From Goku or Thalor, the dust glow and mapped belts depend on elongation, scattering angles, optical depth and observing contrast; quadrature is not by itself a guarantee of maximum forward scattering. Long-exposure imaging reveals three compositional bands: a pale inner arc, a dark central rift, and a reddish-golden outer glow. The full belt occupies a visually significant swath of sky and plays a central role in settler cosmographies, often interpreted as the scar left by a celestial war or the bones of a fallen titan.

The belt also serves as a major source of near-planet impactors and meteor showers, as perturbed fragments occasionally cross into destabilizing resonances with the outer giants. Ongoing surveys continue to map its minor moons, binary asteroids, and transitional centaur-like objects.


## Outer Debris Belt
Spanning from approximately 15 to 30 AU, the **Outer Debris Belt** is the final structured region of the Aethonix system, home to a diffuse population of icy bodies, scattered dwarf planets, and cometary nuclei. Unlike the more compact asteroid belt inside 3.5 AU, the outer belt is dynamically broad and chemically diverse. It is not sharply bounded—instead, its observed15–30 AU structure becomes diffuse, with eccentric bodies defining a soft outer observational edge. The estimated~115 AU heliopause is a separate stellar-wind boundary, not the belt’s continuation or gravitational edge. No trans-belt cloud (analogous to a hypothetical Oort Cloud) has been confirmed; this belt is the system's outer frontier.

Orbits within the belt are widely inclined, many ranging between 2° and 40°, and display significant eccentricity. Unlike the largely planar architecture of the inner planets, this region resembles a torus of low-temperature debris. The failed super-Neptune [[Tsurai]] (~25 M⊕, 12 AU) scatters bodies and can sculpt resonant populations; it can inject as well as remove potential impactors. This interaction has produced a family of resonant bodies known as the **Tsuraids**, found predominantly in 2:1 and 3:2 orbital ratios and exhibiting reference semimajor-axis groups near 15.72 AU(exterior3:2) and 19.05 AU(exterior2:1).

The belt contains thousands of minor bodies and several notable dwarf planets, including:
- **Korelva** – A reddish, heavily cratered dwarf planet with a radius of ~650 km. Displays surface nitrogen and ethane ices, with signs of ancient cryovolcanism.
- **Vastur** – A high-inclination object (~29°) with chaotic rotation, ~470 km in diameter. Appears nearly black in albedo studies, possibly due to accumulated tholin crust.
- **Eyaquon** – A pale-bright ellipsoidal dwarf with a large companion moon and possible brine upwelling fractures, targeted by the robotic probe *Ardent Veil* in 2437.

Most bodies in the belt are composed of water ice, silicates, and exotic volatiles such as methane, carbon dioxide, ammonia, and various nitrile compounds. Simple zero-albedo equilibrium temperatures over 15–30 AU are roughly 82–58 K; colder<40 K sites require exceptional albedo/shadow/emission conditions rather than small size alone, while larger dwarf planets sometimes retain internal heat sufficient to support occasional cryovolcanism or subsurface brine pockets. Spectral analysis indicates a widespread presence of irradiated organics and cosmic-ray processed surface darkening, giving many objects a dusky or reddish hue.

Although the belt sporadically sends cometary material inward, some objects are scattered or removed by Tsurai, while other perturbations can send material inward; most comets are not assumed to be physically intercepted. Nevertheless, active comets from the belt are occasionally observed from [[Goku (exoplanet)]] or [[Velora]], particularly when their tails are ionized by the twin Aethonix suns. No human crews have yet entered the belt, but robotic missions launched from [[Rimeas]] and [[Thalor]] have conducted flybys and sample-return missions.

Culturally, the Outer Belt occupies a liminal status. For planetary settlers, it is often dismissed as *cold, dark, and useless*—a place for theoretical science and far-future explorers. To astrographers, however, it is a crucial boundary zone: a dusty fossil ring of system formation, sculpted by chaos and time. Some frontier enthusiasts speak of the Belt with the reverence of a final unknown, while others regard it as a graveyard of frozen debris, waiting to be forgotten.

**Screens.** The planetary reference uses the explicit binary and eight-planet low-eccentricity screens (5000 years each), which omit satellites. The satellite screen integrates each host with all its declared major moons and a 1.8-M☉ stellar point source, with finite moon masses, nominal eccentricities and inclinations and an initial barycentric reflex adjustment; it omits the binary quadrupole, other planets and hosts, minor moons and rings, J₂, tides, spin, dissipation and climate. It is not a full combined-system integration and does not validate source inclination histories or resonance libration. All seven host models have 1000-year seed-0 runs, and Noctilux, Thalor, Cryodon and Tsurai have second-phase and smaller-step checks, as does Goku's wider pair. A total energy dominated by the host and stars means surviving a trial is not a convergence proof.

A Noctilux layout with Thyxis and Boril at 800,000 and 1,200,000 km failed in the first full four-moon start, and 1,000,000 and 2,000,000 km survived a coarse trial but showed a near-escape in the smaller-step test; the 1,200,000 and 3,000,000-km layout is tested independently. A compact Goku 3:2 hypothesis also failed, which does not exclude every phase-protected resonance. Some surviving models develop appreciable eccentricity excursions, which are tidal and thermal concerns even without escape, so no perpetual low-eccentricity stability is promised, and secular robustness, true resonance angles, coupled all-host perturbations, stellar tides and 4-Gyr survival remain unproved.

**Belts.** The Dawn Lanterns are candidate Rimeas exterior 3:2 families near 2.264 AU, not 60° trojans without a host, and the roughly 3.3-AU groups are associated with Noctilux's interior 4:3 near 3.302 AU. Tsuraid exterior 3:2 and 2:1 groups lie near 15.72 and 19.05 AU. These are plausible locations, not a test-particle occupancy simulation. Ordinary 15–30 AU equilibrium temperatures are about 82–58 K before albedo, and Tsurai can scatter objects inward as well as remove them.

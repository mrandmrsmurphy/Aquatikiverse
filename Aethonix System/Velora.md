**Velora** is a terrestrial exoplanet orbiting the circumbinary star system [[Aethonix]], located approximately 42 light-years from Earth in the constellation [Vela](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vela). It orbits the barycenter of a tight G0V+K4V binary at a distance of 1.2 AU, receiving approximately 1.2 times the solar constant of Earth. Velora is the second planet in the system and lies well within the stable region for P-type orbits.

With a mass of ~1.1 M⊕ and a radius of ~1.05 R⊕, Velora is classified as a warm Earth analog. Its atmosphere maintains a surface pressure of ~0.91 atm and a relatively high albedo (~0.33), dominated by high-altitude clouds and bright terrain. The planet is 62% land-covered, resulting in extensive continental systems and meteorologically active oceans.

The most striking feature of Velora is its extreme axial tilt of approximately 80°, which gives rise to prolonged polar summers and winters. Each geographic pole experiences a long illuminated and a long dark half of the orbital year, with transition details set by geometry; lower polar latitudes have different daylight durations. This axial configuration drives a planet-wide pattern of vigorous seasonal contrasts, dynamic cloud belts, and hemispheric wind reversals.

Velora's rotation period is approximately 22 hours. It possesses a natural satellite with a mass 1.1 times that of Earth’s Moon, which enhances tidal mixing and torques the rotational bulge. Its adopted nominal orbit is 300,000 km; axial precession and obliquity stability require a secular/tidal model rather than a fixed 10,000-year claim.

## Climate and Habitability
Despite its position near the inner edge of the habitable zone, Velora maintains moderate global temperatures (~18 °C mean) due to its reflective albedo and efficient atmospheric circulation. The planet features extreme seasonal dynamics due to its tilt:

- Equatorial and mid-latitude regions support forests and strong seasonal rain belts where ocean currents and topography provide moisture. Year-round equatorial tropical warmth is not imposed solely from the high obliquity.
- Mid-latitudes host deciduous forests and grasslands, with migratory species and strong storm belts whose seasonal paths follow the unusual heating pattern.
- Polar regions alternate between perpetual summer daylight and long winters of darkness, producing melt-seas, tundra, and migratory ecological patterns. A few adapted species endure the polar night in geothermal refugia.

The adopted surface split is **62% land / 38% water**, following this detailed article. At 80° obliquity the annual-mean stellar heating gradient is reversed relative to Earth: the poles receive more annual-mean insolation than the equator, despite long dark winters. Ocean basins, continental interiors and terrain create the specific forests, storm belts, polar melt-seas and cold-season refugia. The 291-K mean is an intended climate reference, not a computed seasonal solution. Exact daylight at the geographic poles follows the tilted orbit; lower latitudes and finite binary geometry have different transition durations.

## Physical basis

At Velora's 80° obliquity the polar annual-mean heating can exceed the equatorial, so forests, rain belts and winter refugia follow seasonal topography and ocean circulation, not an imposed Earth-like latitude rule; high-obliquity dynamic-ocean models give a comparison, not a Velora simulation ([Ferreira et al., climate at high obliquity](https://pog.mit.edu/src/ferreira_climate_high_obliquity_2014.pdf)). The 1.1-lunar-mass moon at 300,000 km gives, with an illustrative J₂ = 0.0013 and C = 0.33, a precession scale of tens of thousands of years, with no proven obliquity stabilization.

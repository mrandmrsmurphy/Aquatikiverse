**Dysmorph** is the outermost planet in the [[Orpheus and Eurydice System]], orbiting the K0V star Orpheus at approximately 1.6 AU. It is a product of post-supernova planetary formation, composed of dense r-process elements and featuring extreme geophysical irregularities, unique atmospheric chemistry, and an energy-rich mantle driven by actinide decay. 

On a system scale, Dysmorph is almost spherical and close to hydrostatic equilibrium. On human scales, however, its gravity field is so uneven that effective surface gravity can change drastically over tens of kilometres, making it a navigational and engineering nightmare. The planet is hostile by terrestrial standards but hosts an established inorganic biosphere based on uranium redox cycles.

## Orbital and Physical Parameters

- **Orbital distance:** ~1.6 AU  
- **Orbital period:** ~2.3 Earth years (~832 Earth days)  
- **Insolation:** ~0.15× Earth  
- **Mass:** ~1.4 Earth masses  
- **Radius:** ~0.95 Earth radii  
- **Average density:** ~9.0 g/cm³  
- **Mean surface gravity:** ~1.6 g  
- **Local effective gravity:** ~0.7 g in low-density basins to >3 g over dense massifs and in gravity traps  

Dysmorph receives significantly less stellar flux than [[Laputa]] and relies heavily on internal energy sources to maintain its geologic and atmospheric activity.

## Shape and Gravitational Field

In bulk, Dysmorph is only mildly oblate and would look like a fairly normal, slightly squashed sphere from space. The problem lies in its internal mass distribution, which is highly uneven.

- The physical surface is close to a spheroid, but the **geoid** (surface of equal gravitational potential) is strongly distorted by dense cores and low-density voids  
- Apparent "bulges" and "dimples" correspond to regions where the geoid is significantly displaced relative to the reference spheroid  
- Local gravity anomalies are caused by dense Os–Ir massifs and actinide-rich cores, as well as low-density halide and void-bearing regions  

Steep gravity gradients define regional topography. Some steep-walled basins and near-vertical shafts above ultra-dense cores produce effective accelerations several times the planetary mean, creating extreme "gravity trap" environments where a misstep can mean being pulled downhill with deadly force.

## Internal Structure

- **Core:** Plutonic core composed of uranium, thorium, osmium, and iridium, with locally produced plutonium in active nuclear zones; asymmetrically distributed, with high-Z element clusters forming deep mass concentrations  
- **Mantle:** Rich in actinides and conductive phases; primary source of radiogenic heat, with partial melt zones surrounding actinide pockets  
- **Crust:** Fragmented, lumpy, and incompletely differentiated, featuring actinide silicates, lanthanide phosphates, heavy-metal inclusions, and brittle halide–ceramic layers  

Dysmorph never fully segregated into a clean iron core plus silicate mantle. Instead, it resembles a partially sorted slag ball in which the densest components have sunk only partway toward the center, freezing in a chaotic supernova-derived composition.

## Heat Budget and Tectonics

- **Internal heat flux:** ~0.3–0.5 W/m², sourced from long-lived isotope decay (U-238, Th-232, K-40; local fission contributes additional episodic heating)  
- **Fission zones:** Naturally occurring Oklo-type reactors in crustal and upper-mantle actinide pockets  
- **Tectonics:** Irregular and stuttering, driven by patchy convection, local melt intrusions, and heat surges from near-critical fission events  
- **Surface activity:** Includes fission venting, radiogenic geysers, actinide-rich lava extrusions, and crustal collapse into gravity traps  

Radiogenic heating is intense enough to maintain widespread partial melts and deep convective plumes. When localized actinide concentrations reach critical geometry, runaway fission produces transient high-flux zones that remobilize the crust, fracture brittle layers, and reset local thermal and mechanical gradients.

## Atmosphere

- **Total pressure:** 2.5–4.0 atm (variable by region and altitude)  
- **Primary composition:**
  - Noble gases: xenon (dominant), krypton, argon, trace radon  
  - Halogens and halides: I₂, CsI, ICl, F₂, HF, and other metal halides  
  - Fission byproducts: Xe-135, Kr-85, Cs-137-bearing aerosols and vapors (short-lived isotopes indicate ongoing criticality)  
  - Secondary gases: CO₂, SO₂, halide-bearing acids and oxidants  

- **Opacity:** Greenhouse opacity from infrared-active molecular species, pressure-dependent effects, and halide aerosols; xenon supplies bulk pressure rather than opacity merely through its mass  
- **Coloration:** Blue-grey to lavender with phosphorescent haze and localized glow above fission basins  
- **Cloud systems:** CsI and metal-halide aerosols, ionized halide fogs with frequent electrical discharges  

The atmosphere is dense, heavy, and chemically aggressive. Halogen-rich clouds, strong greenhouse absorption, and continuous injection of fission byproducts produce a dynamic, radiatively complex climate that is influenced by both stellar insolation and local geothermal hot spots; radiogenic power does not dominate the global incident-energy budget.

## Surface Environment

- **Temperature range:** ~200–450 K (strongly regional and time-dependent)  

**Representative terrain types:**

- Uranyl lakes and SnCl₄/SbCl₃ seas in low-lying basins  
- Os–Ir ridges associated with gravity spikes and steep potential gradients  
- Actinide mesas and Ce–P silicate highlands where crustal material is relatively buoyant  
- Fission plains with glassy deposits, vitrified regolith, and persistent thermal plumes  

**Surface chemistry** is dominated by uranium, cerium, and halide systems. Surfaces often consist of vitrified glass, crusted halides, actinide oxides, and lanthanide-bearing minerals. Liquid phases are typically corrosive halide mixtures rather than water.

The combination of strong gravity in many regions, high chemical reactivity, and periodic fission events makes Dysmorph's surface hazardous for conventional materials and any visiting technology.

## Biosphere
Main article: [[Life on Dysmorph]]

Dysmorph is not expected to support carbon–water biochemistry at the surface. Its native life uses actinide redox cycles and halide solvents, as detailed in [[Life on Dysmorph]]. The exact molecular mechanisms remain speculative.

- **Biochemical solvent analog:** SnCl₄-based and related halide eutectics in the 250–400 K range  
- **Primary metabolism:** U⁴⁺ oxidation to uranyl ions (UO₂²⁺) using Ce⁴⁺ or other high-potential oxidants  
- **Secondary metabolism:** UO₂²⁺ reduction via iodide (I⁻), producing I₂ and closing an iodine redox loop  
- **Cytoplasm analogs:** Halide-rich liquids containing complexed actinides, lanthanides, and heavy metals  
- **Membrane structures:** Halide–phosphate ceramic frameworks incorporating rare earth and actinide chemistry, possibly with dynamic defect lattices instead of organic bilayers  
- **Ecological zonation:** Defined by redox potential, gravity, temperature, and local fission activity rather than by light and water availability  

In this framework, organisms are self-organizing redox reactors distributed within conductive halide fluids, using actinide speciation and halogen cycles to maintain non-equilibrium states. Ecosystems are concentrated around fission basins, redox fronts between uranyl lakes and more oxidized terrains, and deep halide aquifers sheltered from the most extreme surface conditions.

## Notable Geological Features

- **Ir–Os ridges:** Regions where dense osmium–iridium massifs reach close to the surface. Local effective gravity can exceed 3 g on ridge flanks and in adjacent basins, with steep gradients hazardous to both structures and visiting probes.  
- **Fission basins:** Naturally near-critical zones where actinide concentrations sustain periodic or quasi-steady fission. Characterized by high heat flux, intense radiation, vitrified crust, and plumes of fission gases and aerosols.  
- **Cesium lakes:** Halide eutectic pools rich in cesium salts and other heavy halides. These act as chemically stratified reservoirs that may host exotic redox structures in their lower layers.  
- **Thermal lightning fields:** High-energy halide cloud zones above fission plains where thermal updrafts, ionized aerosols, and strong electric fields produce near-continuous lightning and corona discharges.  

These features are mapped primarily by remote sensing and transient probe missions, since long-term surface operations are difficult to maintain.

## Planetary Role

Dysmorph acts as the system’s radiothermal engine and chemical crucible. It converts the residual energy of r-process actinides into heat, volatile production, and complex redox gradients. It is a "failed sphere" in the sense that it never fully settled into a simple layered structure: instead it preserves the chaotic imprint of its supernova origin.

Within the Orpheus–Eurydice system, Dysmorph is the archetypal world of chemical and gravitational asymmetry. Its environment supports complex, inorganic, self-regulating redox systems that blur the line between geology and life, including the Great Uranylization Event described in [[Life on Dysmorph#Origin and Evolution|the biosphere history]] and the emergence of halide autotrophy in actinide-rich basins.
